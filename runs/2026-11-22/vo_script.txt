Under a street in Arlington, a water main is leaking. A leak technician listens, and hears nothing.
A leak that stays underground can go unnoticed for weeks or even months.
Arlington's water utility looks after 1,470 miles of water mains. Before satellites, it mostly relied on residents to report water flowing where it shouldn't be.
Then a team of three went out to listen. They place acoustic gear on valves and over the lines, and triangulate a suspected leak. That is three people for 1,470 miles of main.
So the city tried looking down from orbit. In a pilot, a company called ASTERRA scans the entire city with satellites, for moisture and temperature underground.
The company says its radar looks for the unique signature of drinking water. The city says the technology once looked for water on Mars.
Then an algorithm turns that scan into points of interest. Each one is a pin on the crew's map, a place to go check.
By last November, ASTERRA had handed the city about 340 of those pins. They led to 34 leaks detected and repaired. A pin is not a leak. A crew still drives out to confirm it and repair it.
In July, the utility also tested a second program, Paradigm. Its people check the satellite data against a map of valves and hydrants. One pilot runs on an algorithm. The other runs on people.
When the city tested Paradigm, 7 out of 10 points had leaks. It has helped locate 33 city leaks.
The city estimates ASTERRA saved 1,130,567 gallons of water, and $440,800 in lost water costs. Its conservation supervisor says satellite programs prevent the team from spending entire days searching for leaks.
The utility's year-end report adds a finder that doesn't orbit. Splash, a leak detection dog, sniffs for small amounts of chlorine.
As of last November, both pilots were under evaluation for permanent use. The satellite points. The crew still digs.
