# The clock that has not started

Researchers affiliated with Texas A&M and Houston Methodist published a head-to-head study of two answers to the same practical question: when will an inpatient go home? One answer came from case managers working in usual care. The other came from a commercial clinician-assisted tool integrated with the electronic health record.

At admission, the two methods were close on mean absolute error, though case managers already named the exact date more often. At later checkpoints, the human estimates pulled away. In the analysis labeled twenty-four hours—which used the first estimate recorded in a twelve-to-thirty-six-hour window—79.5 percent of case-manager estimates fell within one day of the actual date, compared with 37.9 percent for the artificial intelligence estimates.

The paper also supplies its own counter-image. Case-manager dates were visible to care teams and may have been partly self-fulfilling; the artificial intelligence dates were accessible in the record but not automatically enabled. The methods were measured inside different workflow conditions.

The single-center analysis measured estimation accuracy, not downstream clinical decisions or patient outcomes, so the authors describe its findings as hypothesis-generating. They call for prospective outcome-based evaluation before judging whether added accuracy in selected groups justifies the extra complexity. That prospective test is the clock that has not started.
