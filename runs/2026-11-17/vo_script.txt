In Midland, a column of drill pipe over two stories tall swings toward the hole. The floorhand pulls back both gloves, because a robot arm already has it.
On this ExxonMobil rig, robotic arms set the pipe in place and connect it to the drill string. Crews on the floor used to help do that by hand.
So the floor gets a fence. The gate carries a sign that reads Red Zone, with a drawing of the Grim Reaper.
Exxon's Bart Cahir says about a third of significant drilling injuries have happened right here, on the rig floor.
Drilling contractor Helmerich and Payne supplies the system. Three arms move pipe weighing roughly 2,000 pounds. One walks each stand to the hole, one lines up the threads, and one racks pipe high in the derrick.
So where did the crew go? Up into a small office on the rig, where a contractor runs the robots with controls on a screen.
That contractor is the driller, who starts each move and watches it, and the arms follow paths planned in advance. The crew works out the exact moves with an Exxon operations team in Houston.
Exxon runs more than 30 rigs in the Permian, and in August, Reuters counted two that were automated. Exxon plans to automate more than half its unconventional fleet by the end of 2028.
The first one already set a pace. It drilled two miles sideways underground in a little over six days, the third fastest time in Exxon's history.
Does a robot take a job? Exxon expects similar staffing, and calls it a force multiplier, not a replacement. It also believes it could prevent roughly a third of high-consequence incidents. Both are company expectations, not counts.
Cahir says people off the floor can plan the next operation instead. So who tightens the pipe now? A robot arm does. A person still decides when.
