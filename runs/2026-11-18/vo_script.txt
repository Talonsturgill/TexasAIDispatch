Picture a crew on Mars, and one of them has severe back pain. A call home for help can take up to 44 minutes to make the round trip.
So who fetches the medical bag? Look again. This is a lab at NASA's Johnson Space Center in Houston, built with habitat mockups, and the one reaching for the bag is a robot.
Google Public Sector built it with the robotics team at Johnson, NASA's Human Research Program and the Jet Propulsion Laboratory.
Its first job sounds simple. Find the medical bag and move it.
Before it touched real hardware, it practised the moves in a physics simulator, where a dropped bag costs nothing.
Its brain is an open model called Pi zero, from a company called Physical Intelligence. Google fine tuned it in just 2.5 hours.
Mars missions need light, low power hardware. So over two years, the team tested about 15 compact devices to run it on.
To know where its own hand is, the robot uses a camera that stands across the room and watches it.
The Jet Propulsion Laboratory is adapting tools built to track satellites in orbit, so the robot can understand the room around it.
In early lab tests at the iMETRO facility, it found and moved the bag 85 percent of the time.
That means some tries missed. It is one task, in one lab, on Earth. Google has not confirmed a space ready robot exists.
The goal is bigger. The robot could fetch and prepare an ultrasound, and take simple voice orders, with astronauts in command.
Even then, it never makes the call. The crew medical officer opens the bag and decides.
Back on Mars, the answer from Earth is still on its way. The bag is already here.
