Since mid-April, fifty bulky trash trucks in Dallas have carried a camera. On their routes, it snaps still photos of the curb.
Not video. Stills. Each goes to software from a company called City Detect, which looks for trouble.
Tall grass. Weeds. Litter. A car on the lawn. Peeling paint. One house got flagged for its chimney paint.
The software blurs faces and license plates. One photo the city released had a black box reading motor vehicle.
The blur slips, though. The company says it works about ninety nine percent of the time. When reporters found a plate it missed, the city asked for every photo to go through the blur again.
In four months, the cameras photographed more than twenty thousand concerns.
Too many to read. So each flagged property gets a blight score from one to four, and the photos are ranked so staff know which to look at first.
A flag is not a ticket. A person reviews it. Staff can do nothing, keep watching, send a courtesy notice, ask for an inspection or call a city crew.
A courtesy notice opens no case and sets no deadline. A citation comes only after an officer inspects in person, gives notice, and the problem is still there.
The city's own maps raise a question. Of the 20,000, about 13,800 came from southern Dallas. Could older blocks with less money for repairs get more scrutiny?
Code compliance director Chris Christian says the trucks cover the whole city, and the camera map matches what residents report almost exactly.
Still, under two percent of southern flags became notices, against more than four percent in the rest of the city. The question stays open.
Now the notices are changing. They will carry a QR code to a city help page, and council members asked for Spanish versions.
The city says about eight in ten owners fix it after a notice. The camera sees the curb. A person decides what happens next.
