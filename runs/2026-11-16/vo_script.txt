In Friendswood, people come outside and look at the creek every day.
Jason Jones, who sits on the local drainage board, says it is almost like PTSD from Harvey and other rain events.
That fear has a ranking. The board's president, Rusty Burkett, calls flooding and drainage the number one threat to lives and property in Galveston County.
Yet until this spring, Jones says, the county had no flood sensors at all.
So in April, the Galveston County Consolidated Drainage District switched on seven radar sensors, six in the Clear Creek watershed and one in the Dickinson Bayou watershed. Each cost less than ten thousand dollars.
One reading is one spot on one creek. So the system adds rainfall totals, stream gauges, weather stations and how soaked the soil already is.
Then AI tools help predict how a flood could develop, even how conditions upstream could affect neighborhoods downstream.
Residents see it as a map. Green means safe. Yellow, flooding possible. Red, flooding likely.
They can sign up for alerts on one creek, and the platform is set to text them when the water rises to a level officials set.
The map does not decide anything. Jones says it helps people choose whether a road is still passable, or whether it is time to leave.
Seven sensors are a start. The district maintains more than 80 miles of creeks, ditches and bayous, and executive director Paige Bailey says the system has already mapped where it needs more gauges.
For now, it covers only Friendswood and League City. People still walk out to look at the creek. Now the creek can answer back.
