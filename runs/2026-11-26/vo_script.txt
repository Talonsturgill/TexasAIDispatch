In a Port Arthur refinery control room, a screen is drawing a steam pressure dip that hasn't happened yet.
The refinery belongs to TotalEnergies, in Jefferson County. It runs about 238,000 barrels of crude a day through 47 units.
One of them is the delayed coker. It uses high heat to crack heavy leftover oil into lighter, more valuable products.
The coker's turbines need high pressure steam, and it has to keep coming.
When a boiler falters, steam line pressure dips. A dip can trip a compressor, and the plant can shut down and flare.
The console operator gets little time to decide which less critical equipment gives up its steam.
So in October 2024, TotalEnergies began testing a Honeywell AI tool on the coker console.
Honeywell's early count, in November 2025, was five potential events, flagged an average of 12 minutes before an alarm.
A later account said its models predicted pressure dips 10 to 18 minutes earlier than before. That is a runway of time to act.
Minutes change the job. Operators acted on at least two of its alerts. Thomas Klein, in process control there, said it was the first time AI at the site caught a situation it had predicted.
Not every model earned its place. One tried to predict the level in the coke drums. It was right 75 percent of the time and was still dropped, because drum switching depends on things outside the model.
Another tool turns past alarms on more than 300 coker tags into examples of what worked before. Klein said it gave new operators high value. It is still in evaluation, and he presented it at Honeywell's own user conference.
Klein admits he was pessimistic. Now he says AI has room on the console, but it is not plug and play, and it needs support.
His advice is to bring operators in from the start. The gauge reads now. The screen reads next. The call is still the operator's.
