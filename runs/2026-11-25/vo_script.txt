A thin curl of smoke rises off the Panhandle grass. Up on a tower, two cameras turn and lock onto it.
There is a reason they are up there. The Smokehouse Creek fire started in Hutchinson County near Stinnett and became the largest wildfire in state history.
It burned roughly 1,058,482 acres. Xcel Energy said its facilities appeared to have been involved.
So Xcel teamed up with a company called Pano AI. By last November, there were 57 camera stations across the Panhandle, each on top of a communication tower.
Each station sweeps a full 360 degrees every minute. It watches about 10 miles in each direction.
Its AI is hunting one thing. Smoke. When it finds a grey smudge on the horizon, it flags it.
A flag is not a fire, though. So a human analyst checks it first.
Then a second tower turns its eyes the same way. Where the two sightlines cross, the map can get exact coordinates.
Pano calls 911. The closest fire department gets the live stream, and a brush truck rolls.
Here is the catch. Xcel's Reeves warns responders that a lot of these alerts will turn out to be nothing.
Most people are okay with that, he says. They want to know everything that is going on.
Some cameras look over towns, though. Those pixelate anything built, houses and all.
Somebody pays for all of it. Xcel paid up front and will ask regulators to recover that cost, and it planned 68 more under a $490 million resiliency plan.
None of it puts out a fire. It buys a head start, and then somebody still has to drive toward the smoke.
