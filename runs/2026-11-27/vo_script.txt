A radiologist reads them in order, top to bottom. So how does the urgent one get to the front?
It starts at the scanner. A router sends the right studies to the AI by itself, and technologists don't touch it. Before, that took setting up each scanner by hand.
Then an AI tool called Aidoc reads the images. It flags a possible problem and paints a heat map on the spot it means.
When the concern looks significant, that exam jumps to the top of the radiologist's worklist.
Some scans can't wait. A pulmonary embolism is a blood clot that breaks loose and travels to the lungs. It can be deadly.
With a stroke, one study estimates a typical patient loses 1.9 million neurons for every minute it goes untreated.
So Houston Methodist started with 18 super users testing it, then opened it to roughly 100 radiologists in June.
Radiologists were skeptical at first. Ginny Torno, who leads the rollout, says even the most skeptical one called it a great tool.
She also says it isn't perfect, and Aidoc is upfront about that. A flag is a reason to look first, never the answer.
So the radiologist still reads the scan and makes the call. Every time Aidoc gets feedback, it makes a change.
Next, the hospital plans phone alerts for stroke and pulmonary embolism teams, built for triage, not diagnosis. Clinicians would see the images on their phones and call in a response team right away.
