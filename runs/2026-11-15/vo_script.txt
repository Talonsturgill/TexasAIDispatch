A truck rolls into Port Houston for one orange container. It sits at the bottom of a stack, buried under other boxes.
So the crane has to dig, lifting box after box to reach it, while the driver waits below.
Port Houston was managing 142 of these rubber-tired gantry cranes at its Barbours Cut and Bayport terminals, on the Houston Ship Channel.
Those terminals see up to 12,000 truck visits a day, and a driver picking up comes for one particular box.
The pressure keeps building. 2025 was a record year for containers at Port Houston, and volumes are forecast to keep growing.
So which move should a crane make next? Software from a company called Kaleris now helps decide.
It is called RTG Optimization. It plans the order of crane moves to avoid blocked stacks, inside the terminal's own operating system.
It sends cranes where the demand is, using better data on what the yard needs.
It didn't start everywhere at once. The rollout began with truck moves in one small section of the yard.
Lessons from those early stages became custom filters and workflow changes that improved the operators' experience.
People still run the cranes. Port Houston operations chief Ryan Mariacher says the system works in tandem with the skilled labor of the ILA, the International Longshoremen's Association.
Kaleris reports up to a 20 percent gain in yard crane productivity. That is the company's own figure, and up to means a ceiling.
So back to that truck. This time the orange box is already on top. Same crane, same hands, a better next move.
