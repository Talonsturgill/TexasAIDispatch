In June, federal, state and local agencies flew drones at a demonstration in Jim Wells County, South Texas. They were hunting a flesh-eating fly that is moving north over the border.
The idea is simple. Drones, cameras and artificial intelligence could spot an injured or infested animal in country too rough to ride into. That is what the demonstration set out to show.
Michael Schmoyer of the USDA's animal health agency said the tools already work well on feral hogs. So the question was whether they could work on screwworm too.
Spotters in the air and on the ground will rely on cameras, infrared, night vision, heat sensors, dogs and AI. When a hide shows an open wound, the technology takes a closer look to see if the fly is attacking.
John Bellinger, a lead advisor to the Agriculture Secretary, has seen it before. He treated screwworm on calves at his grandfather's place in Wilson County. At the demonstration, he said it's back, and the country has to solve it.
Spectrum News reported infected cases in Zavala, La Salle and Gillespie counties. The animals were cattle, a goat and a dog.
Bellinger called wildlife one of the biggest challenges. White-tailed deer and feral hogs can become hosts without anyone knowing they were hurt.
So officials call technology one part of the plan. The most effective weapon, they say, is still the sterile fly program.
Bellinger said the country needs 400 million sterile flies in 2027. As of June, a sterile fly plant in Edinburg was expected to open early that year.
USDA has help from Customs and Border Protection. KRGV reported that the drones use AI to flag animals that may be infected.
For now, producers are urged to inspect their animals more often and report any suspected case.
The drone can point. Somebody still has to walk out and look.
