# Brands and ads — the commercial Texas, drawn as vernacular and never as a mark

Old signs, drink brands, restaurant trade dress and roadside advertising are a deep nostalgia
lever, and almost every one is a LIVE trademark. This file is the research on twenty-nine of them,
kept in the only shape that is safe to use: what is PROTECTED (never draw) beside the DRAWABLE
VERNACULAR (the form the protected thing sits on, which is free and carries the recognition).

**The rule is the whole file: draw the vernacular, never the mark.** A striped A-frame reads as a
Texas burger stand with no word on it, because the form was built to be seen from a mile. A tall
rotating red neon horse reads as Dallas whether or not it ever says a brand. Draw those. Never the
wordmark, the logo, the exact trade-dress color, or the character.

These are not built as artifacts. This is the brief for when they are, and the durable record of
research that otherwise lived only in a scratch file. Cross-check `FOOTBALL.md` and `LANDMARKS.md`,
which carry their own overlapping marks (the cooler, the Pegasus, the Dr Pepper museum).

## Dr Pepper

**What it is.** The Dr Pepper soft drink and its 10-2-4 branding system. Formulated in the 1880s by pharmacist Charles Alderton at Morrison's Old Corner Drug Store in Waco, Texas, and named/marketed by store owner Wade Morrison. First marketed nationally in 1904 at the Louisiana Purchase Exposition (St. Louis World's Fair).

**Why it lands.** A soda-fountain origin story that is verifiably Texan rather than manufactured nostalgia, plus the 'Dr Pepper Time' habit (10, 2, and 4 o'clock breaks), reinforced by a WWII-era radio show, The 10-2-4 Ranch, and a 1960s jingle set to the tune of 'The Glow-Worm'.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** The Dr Pepper wordmark (the period after 'Dr' was used intermittently until 1950, then permanently dropped for legibility), the specific 10-2-4 clock-face logo device, and the filed label typography/colors.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** A generic soda-fountain clock face reading any three arbitrary hours; a generic '[Name] Time' mid-century soft-drink wordmark lockup; a plain straight-sided glass soda-bottle silhouette; a soda-jerk drugstore-counter scene (marble counter, round stools, brass dispenser arms) with no brand lettering on the glassware.

**Geometry.** Straight-sided glass bottle silhouette typical of early 20th-century bottling; no specific verified bottle dimension found this session (UNCERTAIN). The 1906 Artesian Manufacturing and Bottling Company building in Waco (first built specifically to bottle Dr Pepper, now the Dr Pepper Museum) is a real brick industrial facade; no verified building dimensions this session (UNCERTAIN).

**Palette.** The drink itself is described in sourced material only as 'caramel' colored. UNCERTAIN on any specific label/logo color (maroon, burgundy, etc.) -- not independently verified this session.

**Era markers.** Period after 'Dr' present intermittently through 1950, dropped permanently after. Pre-1950 vs post-1950 is a usable dating convention for an invented period soda wordmark, not for reproducing the real one.

**Regional.** Waco, Texas (birthplace); statewide and eventually national distribution.

**Variants.** 1880s drugstore soda-fountain counter scene WWII-era radio-ranch tie-in staging (generic ranch-radio set) 1960s clock/timepiece motif referencing the jingle 1906 brick Artesian bottling-building exterior

**Scene role.** Background prop bridging 'small-town Texas commerce' and 'soda-fountain nostalgia' beats.

## Dublin Dr Pepper

**What it is.** The independent Dublin, Texas bottling plant, the oldest Dr Pepper bottler, operating from 1891 and the first franchise bottler when Dr Pepper began franchising in 1925 under owner Sam Houston Prim. A 2011-2012 trademark lawsuit from Dr Pepper Snapple Group over cane-sugar formula and territory forced removal of the Dr Pepper name; the plant continues as Dublin Bottling Works, producing 18 other cane-sugar sodas.

**Why it lands.** A small West Texas town kept an original cane-sugar formula alive for decades after the industry switched to corn syrup, built a pilgrimage soda-fountain and museum around it, then lost the name in a lawsuit brought by its own parent brand -- a very Texan small-town-versus-corporation memory.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** The retired 'Dublin Dr Pepper' name/wordmark itself, the Dr Pepper trademark and 10-2-4 device generally, and any current Dublin Bottling Works labels/trademarks.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** A generic small-town brick bottling-plant facade with vintage bottling-line machinery visible through windows; a generic 'Old [Name]'s Soda Shop' fountain counter; stacked wooden or metal soda-crate walls; a generic small-town Texas main-street storefront housing a local bottler/museum. None of these forms are protectable, only the specific brand words on them are.

**Geometry.** UNCERTAIN on precise plant dimensions (not verified this session). Sourced detail: much of the machinery dates to the 1930s, and the plant runs only once a month to refill roughly 2,000 recirculating glass bottles -- a useful small-scale, low-throughput detail for staging the interior.

**Palette.** Red brick construction confirmed generically for this building type; UNCERTAIN on any specific trim or signage color (not verified this session).

**Era markers.** 1930s-vintage bottling machinery still in use; 2012 name change marks a clean before/after point for a Dublin-set scene.

**Regional.** Dublin, Texas (Erath County, North-Central Texas).

**Variants.** working bottling-hall interior with belts, glass bottles, and crates storefront soda-shop fountain counter wide roadside 'pilgrimage stop' shot with parked cars and a generic hand-lettered SODA sign an empty or repainted sign board referencing the 2012 name-loss, used sparingly as a bittersweet beat

**Scene role.** A single specific-place beat for a 'Texas invented it and kept it real' story arc; use once per program, not as a recurring background.

## Big Red

**What it is.** Big Red cream soda, created in 1937 in Waco, Texas by Grover C. Thomsen, R.H. Roark, and Robert Montes; originally marketed as 'Sun Tang Red Cream Soda' before renaming in 1959 and again to 'Big Red' in 1969.

**Why it lands.** A specific lemon-orange-vanilla flavor (not bubble gum, despite the common assumption) that became reflexive shorthand for Central and South Texas Tex-Mex meals -- a red soda ordered alongside barbacoa or brisket plates.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** The Big Red wordmark, label design, and trade dress as currently held by its owner; exact current corporate ownership was not independently verified this session (UNCERTAIN).

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** The generic form is simply 'a red soda in a clear glass bottle or can' -- the drink's own bright red color does the recognition work, so a sweating glass bottle of unbranded red soda on a taco-stand table or in a gas-station cooler is fully free to draw as long as no wordmark appears.

**Geometry.** Standard soft-drink bottle/can silhouette; no specific dimension verified this session (UNCERTAIN).

**Palette.** Red liquid, red bottles and cans, confirmed by source. Exact hue (cherry-red vs. cream-red) not verified to a specific value this session -- treat as a bright, warm red family.

**Era markers.** Pre-1959 'Sun Tang Red Cream Soda' era name versus post-1969 'Big Red' era name, useful only as a dating fact, not for lettering a real can.

**Regional.** Waco origin; strongest cultural association with Central and South Texas, though the source also documents an early foothold in Louisville, Kentucky, so it is not a purely Texas-exclusive history.

**Variants.** sweating bottle on a taco-truck counter red soda beside a plate of barbacoa or brisket a gas-station cooler full of identical red bottles a child's party table with the drink as a color accent

**Scene role.** Color-accent prop for food and gathering scenes, not a standalone subject.

## Blue Bell

**What it is.** Blue Bell Creameries, founded 1907 in Brenham, Texas as the Brenham Creamery Company (butter first, ice cream from 1911); renamed Blue Bell in 1930 after the Texas bluebell wildflower favored by E.F. Kruse, who took over the struggling company in 1919 and built it into 'the little creamery in Brenham.'

**Why it lands.** A genuine, verifiable point of difference (true half-gallon cartons when competitors shrank to 48-56 oz) plus small-town-creamery mythology built around one family's ownership since 1919.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** The Blue Bell wordmark and its logo imagery, and label typography/colors as filed by the company.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** A generic rectangular half-gallon paperboard ice-cream carton silhouette (an unownable package shape); a white clapboard dairy building; a black-and-white-patched dairy-cow silhouette in a pasture (cow coat patterns are not ownable); a generic vintage delivery truck.

**Geometry.** Half-gallon (64 fl oz / 1.89 L) carton proportions are the brand's documented differentiator from competitors' reduced 48-56 fl oz containers -- a generic brick-shaped carton at this larger scale reads as 'old-style dairy carton' without needing any label art.

**Palette.** UNCERTAIN on Blue Bell's specific logo colors -- not verified this session (the company's own site and image-description pages were unreachable). Generic dairy-scene palette (cream/white, pasture green) is safe.

**Era markers.** 1907-1911 transition from butter-only to ice cream production; 1930 renaming to Blue Bell.

**Regional.** Brenham, Texas (Washington County), with advertising imagery leaning on small-town, rural Central Texas settings.

**Variants.** half-gallon carton silhouette in hand at a summer scene pastoral dairy-farm establishing shot vintage delivery-truck scene white clapboard creamery building exterior

**Scene role.** Nostalgia/summer prop; pairs with small-town Texas imagery generally.

## Whataburger

**What it is.** Whataburger, founded August 8, 1950 in Corpus Christi, Texas by Harmon Dobson and Paul Burton. The orange-and-white striped A-frame building debuted in 1961 at store #24 in Odessa; the 'Flying W' logo was commissioned in 1967, reflecting Dobson's own love of aviation.

**Why it lands.** A silhouette readable at highway speed, a proudly Texan late-night ritual (Corpus Christi locations went 24-hour by 1982), and a design origin rooted in real aviation practice rather than arbitrary branding.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** The 'Flying W' logo, the Whataburger wordmark, and the specific orange-and-white A-frame trade dress as used by the company.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** The A-FRAME BUILDING TYPE ITSELF -- a steep triangular gable roof over a low rectangular dining box -- is a generic mid-century roadside-restaurant silhouette used across many 1950s-60s chains and independents; a striped roof in any two-color combination is a free generic decorative device on that generic form; an unbranded 24-hour Texas roadside burger stand is a free scene to stage.

**Geometry.** Steep-pitch A-frame gable sitting atop a low one-story rectangular volume; the silhouette rule is a continuous diagonal roofline from a low eave to a ridge cap. No verified standard footprint or roof-pitch angle this session (UNCERTAIN); the documented 1999 'Whataburger by the Bay' flagship is an outsized 6,000 sq ft exception, not the standard unit.

**Palette.** Orange and white is the SPECIFIC protected combination. Source confirms the choice was deliberate: orange and white are 'traditionally the colors used on many airport structures,' chosen for visibility from the air, reflecting Dobson's aviation background. For a generic A-frame prop, use a different two-color stripe pairing (red-and-white, teal-and-white, brown-and-tan) to stay clearly generic.

**Era markers.** 1961 origin of the striped A-frame; 1974 'Modern A-Frame' added a drive-through lane and larger dining room -- pure walk-up A-frame reads as early 1960s, A-frame-plus-drive-through reads as post-1974.

**Regional.** Originated Corpus Christi; ubiquitous statewide by the 1970s-80s.

**Variants.** dusk/lit A-frame with parking lot, a night-owl beat daytime highway-shoulder wide shot 1974-style A-frame with an added drive-through lane early-1960s walk-up counter interior with no dining room

**Scene role.** Highway/roadside establishing prop; strong as a silhouette in wide shots or a driving-by background beat.

## Heb Texas Grocery

**What it is.** H-E-B, founded November 26, 1905 in Kerrville by Florence Butt as 'Mrs. C.C. Butt's Staple and Fancy Grocery.' Renamed for son Howard Edward Butt, who introduced self-service cash-and-carry in 1922. Now Texas's dominant home-grown grocery chain, with store banners including H-E-B Plus!, Central Market, Mi Tienda, Joe V's Smart Shop, and True Texas BBQ.

**Why it lands.** H-E-B functions closer to civic infrastructure than retail in much of Texas (a widely repeated reputation for disaster-relief logistics), so the emotional register is regional trust and loyalty rather than a single nostalgic sign.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** The H-E-B wordmark/logo, and the specific store-banner names (H-E-B Plus!, Central Market, Mi Tienda, Joe V's Smart Shop, True Texas BBQ) and their trade dress.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** The generic Texas/Southwest supermarket type: a large single-story big-box grocery with a covered front colonnade sheltering cart corrals, generic bilingual aisle and produce signage reflecting South Texas retail bilingualism, a generic striped produce-department awning. This massing and layout is a broadly shared Sun Belt supermarket convention, not owned by any one chain.

**Geometry.** Single-story big-box massing with a covered entry arcade. UNCERTAIN on any specific footprint or facade proportions -- not verified this session.

**Palette.** UNCERTAIN on H-E-B's specific corporate colors -- not verified this session; sourced material covered company history but never described the logo's appearance. Use only a generic Sun Belt retail palette (stucco tan/beige, red-tile accents) rather than guessing at the real brand color.

**Era markers.** 1922 self-service conversion is a clean dividing line between a pre-1922 counter-service general-store staging and a post-1922 self-service supermarket-aisle staging.

**Regional.** Kerrville origin (Texas Hill Country); dominant statewide, with documented expansion into northern Mexico.

**Variants.** big-box exterior with cart corral bilingual produce-aisle interior small-town-scaled store versus big-city Central-Market-scaled store checkout-lane conveyor-belt slice-of-life beat

**Scene role.** Civic/errand-day establishing beat for everyday-life transitions.

## Bucees Billboard

**What it is.** Buc-ee's, founded 1982 in Clute, Texas by Arch 'Beaver' Aplin III and Don Wasek; grew from an ordinary convenience store into 'mega' travel centers starting with Luling in 2003 (56 locations across 13 states per source). Famous for a beaver mascot in a yellow-circle black-outline roundel and an aggressive highway billboard campaign of pun-based ads placed hundreds of miles from the nearest store.

**Why it lands.** The billboard gag turns a road trip into an anticipation-building countdown across hundreds of miles before you ever reach the store, layered onto genuine superlative-chasing (the world's longest car wash at 255 ft in Katy, oversized snack walls, famously clean restrooms).

**PROTECTED, never draw.** The Buc-ee's wordmark, the specific cartoon beaver mascot in its yellow-circle black-ring roundel, and the company's actively litigated trade dress around that roundel format (the company is documented to have sued competitors over similar aesthetics).

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** The oversized roadside pun-billboard AS A FORMAT (a wide sun-bleached board with a short all-caps joke and a mileage callout) is a century-old generic outdoor-advertising convention owned by no one; a generic circular animal-mascot badge (round frame, ring border, a big-toothed animal face) is a free generic mascot LAYOUT as long as the specific beaver likeness is not copied and the badge is not rendered in the specific yellow-and-black combination; the 'mega travel-center' building type (very long, low, big-box with a large fuel-pump forecourt and a tall pylon sign) is a free generic form.

**Geometry.** The travel-center building type is documented at an extreme scale (up to roughly 75,000 sq ft for the largest real example), useful as an exaggeration reference for a 'biggest in Texas' staging. UNCERTAIN on any standard billboard panel dimensions this session.

**Palette.** Yellow-and-black is the SPECIFIC protected roundel combination; a generic pun-billboard prop carries no fixed brand color and is safe in any palette a scene needs.

**Era markers.** Pre-2003 Buc-ee's were ordinary-scale convenience stores; the 'mega travel center' scale is a post-2003 phenomenon -- useful for an origin-story beat at smaller, humbler scale versus a present-day beat at exaggerated scale.

**Regional.** Clute/Lake Jackson origin (Texas Gulf Coast); now a multi-state highway-corridor phenomenon, so it reads as Texas-born rather than Texas-exclusive.

**Variants.** a receding series of pun-billboards counting down highway miles, a driving-montage beat a wall-length lit fuel-pump forecourt at dusk an interior snack-wall wide shot a single deadpan pun-board alone in flat farmland with no store in sight

**Scene role.** Road-trip/highway-driving beat; works well as a repeating gag across multiple frames (a mile-marker countdown).

## Shiner

**What it is.** Shiner beer, brewed by Spoetzl Brewery, founded 1909 in Shiner, Texas by German and Czech immigrant families as the Shiner Brewing Association. Bavarian-trained brewmaster Kosmos Spoetzl bought the brewery in 1915, lending his name while keeping the 'Shiner' product name. The brewery survived Prohibition by selling ice and near beer; Shiner Bock, the flagship, follows the German/Bavarian tradition of a dark Lenten lager.

**Why it lands.** The oldest independent brewery in Texas, still operating in the same small town it started in, carrying a German-Czech immigrant heritage distinct from the cowboy-coded thread of Texas identity; Shiner Bock is the default 'local beer' order across Central Texas dance halls and barbecue joints.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** The Shiner wordmark and label design, and the Spoetzl Brewery name and any trademarked building signage.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** The small-town German-Czech Texas brewery/dance-hall building type generically: a modest brick industrial brewhouse with a large hand-painted brick-wall advertisement (blank or invented lettering, not a specific brand's), and the wider wood-frame, tin-roofed, string-lit Central Texas dance-hall form shared across the German-heritage settlement belt (New Braunfels, Fredericksburg, and similar towns).

**Geometry.** UNCERTAIN on specific brewery building dimensions -- not verified this session (the brewery's own site returned a 403 error). Generic small-town industrial brewhouse massing (one to three stories, tall narrow window bays typical of early-1900s brewery architecture) is safe to draw.

**Palette.** UNCERTAIN on Shiner's specific label colors -- the brewery's own site (shiner.com) returned HTTP 403 Forbidden this session and could not be verified. Do not render the actual label. Safe generic materials: red brick, dark timber, warm string-light glow for a dance-hall setting.

**Era markers.** 1909 founding; 1920-1933 Prohibition-era survival by selling ice and near beer, a distinct hard-times beat; 1989 ownership change under Carlos Alvarez marks the shift from small regional brewery (36,000 barrels in 1990) to fast-growing craft player (over 100,000 barrels by 1994).

**Regional.** Shiner, Texas (Lavaca County), in the German-Czech settlement belt of Central Texas.

**Variants.** brewhouse exterior with a blank brick gable-end ad panel a string-lit dance-hall scene a Lenten/dark-lager seasonal beat using a generic goat motif (a traditional bock-beer symbol in German brewing broadly, not Shiner-specific) a Prohibition-era 'selling ice' survival beat

**Scene role.** Small-town craft-and-heritage prop, pairs with German-Czech Hill Country architecture beats.

## Lone Star

**What it is.** Lone Star Beer. An original Lone Star Brewing Company operated in San Antonio from 1884 (as Alamo Brewing Co.) until 1919; an unrelated company under the same name, also San Antonio, began brewing 'Lone Star Beer' in 1940. Marketed for decades as 'The National Beer of Texas.' Ownership passed through Stroh, then Pabst (1999); production today is at Anheuser-Busch's Houston plant.

**Why it lands.** A documented 1970s tie to the Austin 'cosmic cowboy' and outlaw-country music scene gave it a counter-cultural-but-still-Texan identity, reinforced by repeated film and TV appearances (Urban Cowboy, American Sniper, Friday Night Lights, True Detective) that made it screen shorthand for 'this is Texas.'

**PROTECTED, never draw.** The Lone Star wordmark, star device, label design, and trade dress as currently owned by Pabst.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** The five-pointed star itself is an unownable, ubiquitous Texas civic symbol (it is on the state flag), so star motifs on a beer sign, tap handle, or bar wall are generic Texas iconography as long as the specific label artwork and typography are not copied. A generic backlit tin bar sign (a shallow lit box, round or rectangular, with an invented word or symbol) is also a free generic prop.

**Geometry.** UNCERTAIN on any specific can, bottle, or sign dimension -- not verified this session. A generic backlit bar-sign cabinet at roughly picture-frame scale is a safe generic prop size.

**Palette.** UNCERTAIN on Lone Star's specific label colors -- not described in any source reached this session. Use a generic warm white/amber sign glow regardless of brand rather than guessing at the real palette.

**Era markers.** The 1970s Austin outlaw-country/music-scene association is the strongest single era-tell for evoking Lone Star without using the mark -- pair a generic longneck-bottle silhouette with a dance-hall or outdoor-concert setting from that decade.

**Regional.** San Antonio origin; culturally coded as broadly 'Texan' rather than tied to one region, reinforced nationally through film and TV.

**Variants.** a backlit bar-window sign glow (unlettered box) as a night-scene light source a longneck bottle silhouette on a dance-hall table a star motif worked into a generic tooled-leather or wrought-iron ornament, not the beer brand specifically an outlaw-country concert-crowd beat referencing the 1970s association generically

**Scene role.** Night/bar-scene light source and a fast 'this is Texas' atmosphere cue without needing any wordmark.

## Texas Beer Sign

**What it is.** The generic backlit or painted-tin beer-brand sign as a fixture of Texas bars, ice houses, and dance halls -- a genre evidenced by Lone Star, Shiner, and San Antonio's Pearl Brewing (founded 1883 as part of a lineage back to the J.B. Behloradsky and City Brewery; 'Pearl' named for foam bubbles resembling pearls; its distinctive 'Triple-X' circle quality-mark logo dates to the 1880s City Brewery era, survived Prohibition as Alamo Foods, returned after 1933, and stayed prominent until the brewery's 2001 closure, when the site became Hotel Emma).

**Why it lands.** These signs are the wallpaper of Texas nightlife memory -- the specific brand matters less than the warm colored glow, the buzz of an old transformer, and a recognizable shape glimpsed in a dark window from the street.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** Any specific brewery's actual wordmark, star, or symbol device rendered accurately -- Lone Star's star-and-name lockup, Shiner's label, and Pearl's Triple-X circle mark specifically.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** A generic backlit sign cabinet as an object -- a shallow internally lit box, round or rectangular, hung in a window or over a bar, its face a plain glowing color field -- is a free, brand-neutral prop. A circle-and-letters badge layout (echoing the general convention Pearl's Triple-X and countless other beer badges use) is a generic layout, not a specific mark, as long as the letters or symbol inside are invented.

**Geometry.** A simple flat or slightly convex disc or box, proportioned roughly like a large clock face, hung flush to a window or wall plane. No verified standard dimension this session (UNCERTAIN).

**Palette.** Warm amber/white backlight glow is the universal generic. If adding a color field behind the glow, any single saturated hue reads as 'beer sign' without copying a specific brand -- avoid pairing red-white-blue (evokes Lone Star) or the specific Pearl combination if the intent is strictly generic.

**Era markers.** Pre-1930s flat, non-illuminated embossed or painted tin signs, versus mid-century electric light-box signs (the broader rise of neon and electrical signage from the 1930s-40s), versus 1970s-80s flat plastic backlit signage -- three distinct silhouette/technology eras useful for dating a scene.

**Regional.** Statewide; the strongest documented lineage is San Antonio (Lone Star, Pearl) and the Central Texas German-Czech towns (Shiner).

**Variants.** a window full of several differently shaped glowing signs at night, a skyline-of-glows composition a single sign mounted over a back bar mirror a dusty daylight tin sign nailed to an ice-house screen wall a buzzing flickering neon tube outline beside a steady flat plastic light box, contrasting two eras in one frame

**Scene role.** Ambient night-scene lighting and a fast 'we are in a Texas bar' establishing cue.

## Fritos Corn Chip

**What it is.** Fritos corn chips. In July 1932, C.E. Doolin purchased a recipe, a custom potato ricer, and 19 retail accounts for $100 from Gustavo Olguin, a San Antonio-based seller originally from Oaxaca, Mexico. The Doolin family founded Frito Corporation that September, grew from a garage operation to fifty-plus plants by 1955, merged with H.W. Lay and Company in 1961 to form Frito-Lay, then merged with Pepsi-Cola in 1965 to form PepsiCo.

**Why it lands.** A fried-masa corn chip that is a genuine, traceable Texas invention with a real immigrant-recipe origin, not manufactured marketing myth, and the base ingredient of later Texas snack-stand food built on top of it.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** The Fritos wordmark and bag trade dress, and specifically the retired Frito Kid mascot (1952-1967) and the Frito Bandito mascot (1967 to roughly 1971, discontinued amid complaints that the character perpetuated a racist stereotype) -- the Bandito in particular is a case for 'do not revive,' not just a trademark case.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** The corn chip's physical form is a generic, unownable food-item silhouette: a thick, curled, rough-edged triangular-to-irregular fried-masa shape. A generic gusseted snack-bag silhouette (tall rectangle, wider flat base, twist-top or heat-seal closure) is a free generic package form. A generic chip-and-dip or chip-and-chili scene is free to stage.

**Geometry.** An individual chip at roughly 2 to 3 inches is a familiar general food-object scale, not a verified Frito-specific spec (UNCERTAIN); the bag is a standard gusseted snack-bag shape.

**Palette.** UNCERTAIN on any specific Fritos bag color values -- not verified this session. The corn chip's own golden-yellow, toasted-corn color is a safe generic food color, not a trademark.

**Era markers.** 1932 garage-operation origin is a useful 'handmade beginning' beat; the 1961 Frito-Lay and 1965 PepsiCo mergers mark the shift from a Texas-regional company to a national conglomerate, useful for contrasting local-origin versus corporate-scale beats.

**Regional.** San Antonio (recipe origin) and Dallas (early plant expansion).

**Variants.** a single fried chip in extreme close-up, a texture study a 'walking taco' scene: a split-open chip bag with chili and toppings piled inside, a genuine Texas/Southwest snack-stand convention a mid-century snack bag on a diner counter a 1930s hand-fried small-batch kitchen scene referencing the recipe's origin

**Scene role.** Food/snack-culture prop for county-fair, drive-in, or backyard-gathering beats.

## Lubys Cafeteria Line

Grew to over 200 locations across 11 states by 1996, then declined sharply after 2015, filed for liquidation following COVID-19 lockdowns in 2020, with 32 locations sold to Flying Food Group in 2021.

**Why it lands.** The cafeteria SERVING FORMAT itself, more than the Luby's brand specifically, is the real nostalgia object -- a tray-slide steam-table line where you choose a wedge of pie before reaching the entrees, a 'Sunday after church' ritual for generations of Texans. The chain's LuAnn Platter combo meal is documented as the direct inspiration for King of the Hill's Luanne Platter character, a nice verified pop-culture echo.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** The Luby's wordmark/logo and any specific interior trade dress the company registered.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** The cafeteria serving line itself is a generic mid-century American food-service format, not ownable by any one chain: a stainless-steel tray-slide rail, a sneeze-guard glass canopy over steam-table wells, small angled placard price signs, a stack of plain plastic trays, a wall of small glass dishes holding pre-plated salads and desserts. This assembly was shared by many chains and countless independents and is fully free to draw undecorated.

**Geometry.** A single-file line rail at roughly counter height runs the length of a steam table, with sneeze-guard glass at about head height above the trays. These are familiar generic proportions, not sourced measurements this session (UNCERTAIN).

**Palette.** UNCERTAIN on any Luby's-specific interior color scheme -- not verified this session. Generic cafeteria palette is safe: stainless steel, beige or cream trays, warm incandescent or fluorescent overhead light.

**Era markers.** 1947-1960s independent single-city cafeterias read smaller and plainer; 1970s-90s mall and strip-mall chain cafeterias read larger and more standardized -- a useful scale-based era tell.

**Regional.** San Antonio origin; expanded statewide and into the broader South/Southwest.

**Variants.** the tray-slide line in long receding perspective, a classic composition a family seated at a laminate table with loaded trays a dessert case close-up: jello cups, pie wedges, pudding an exterior strip-mall cafeteria storefront with large picture windows and a blank marquee

**Scene role.** A 'Sunday family meal' or small-town social-hub interior beat.

## Southwest Love Field

**What it is.** Southwest Airlines, founded March 9, 1967 by Herb Kelleher and Rollin King as Air Southwest Co., renamed Southwest Airlines in 1971 when it began flying intrastate Texas routes (Dallas-Houston-San Antonio) from its enduring home base, Dallas Love Field. The original 1971-2001 livery, called Desert Gold, used gold, red, and orange color bands separated by white pinstripes.

**Why it lands.** Southwest's Love Field era is Texas's own scrappy-underdog airline legend, with a documented playful crew culture; the specific gold-red-orange livery era is a distinct color memory from the airline's later blue-and-yellow 'Heart' identity.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** The current Southwest wordmark and Heart logo (2014-present), and the specific historical livery color-block and pinstripe arrangements as applied trade dress.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** The horizontal color-band fuselage layout separated by thin contrasting pinstripes is a generic commercial-aviation livery CONVENTION used by dozens of unrelated airlines for decades; a striped fuselage in an invented color combination (not gold-red-orange) reads as '1970s jet livery' without copying Southwest. The Love Field terminal itself can be drawn as an anonymous mid-century airport-concourse form (jet bridges, tarmac, a control-tower silhouette).

**Geometry.** The airline flew almost exclusively Boeing 737-family aircraft (with a brief late-1970s to early-1980s Boeing 727 exception per source) -- draw a generic narrow-body twin-engine jet silhouette rather than a specific registered livery. No specific fuselage proportions verified beyond this general airframe family.

**Palette.** The original 1971-2001 scheme is documented specifically as gold, red, and orange bands with white pinstripes ('Desert Gold'). **Why it lands.** The drive-in turned ordering food into a small piece of theater performed at your own car window -- a conversation with a disembodied speaker-box voice, then a tray hooked over the rolled-down glass, with carhops (sometimes on skates) as the visible service layer -- and Texas can claim the format's actual 1921 origin point, not just a later chain's growth.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** Any specific real chain's actual menu-board graphic design, wordmark, and mascot -- for example Sonic's current logo and stall-numbering system.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** The entire drive-in service apparatus as a set of objects is generic: a canopy-covered row of parking stalls angled toward a central building, a car-side speaker/intercom post at each stall (a small pedestal with a grille and button, a generic mid-century intercom object), a carhop figure on roller skates carrying a tray, a car-window tray that hooks over rolled-down glass. Every piece of this apparatus is a widely shared, decades-old generic restaurant-service convention used historically by many chains and independents alike, fully free to draw undecorated. A menu board itself -- a flat panel with rows of item names and prices -- is a generic information-display object regardless of restaurant.

**Geometry.** A canopy spans roughly car-height-plus-clearance over a single row of diagonally angled parking stalls; a speaker post sits at roughly a seated driver's window height beside each stall. These are familiar generic scales, not sourced measurements this session (UNCERTAIN).

**Palette.** UNCERTAIN on any specific chain's canopy color scheme -- not verified this session. A safe generic mid-century drive-in palette uses bright primary-color canopy stripes and chrome trim, a broadly shared period convention rather than one brand's mark.

**Era markers.** A speaker-box-and-carhop format (1950s-70s heyday) versus a modern touchscreen or intercom-app ordering kiosk (2000s onward) is a clean generic era-tell. Roller skates specifically mark the classic mid-century-to-modern-revival carhop image, since the practice is documented as still active and competitively judged at some locations today, not purely historical.

**Regional.** Format-originating in Dallas (Kirby's Pig Stand, 1921); the carhop-speaker-box small-town version is especially dense across Oklahoma and Texas following Sonic's documented 1960s-70s regional expansion.

**Variants.** a canopy-and-stalls wide shot at dusk, a neon or incandescent glow beat a speaker-post close-up as an object-study prop a roller-skating carhop mid-delivery, a motion beat a red-tiled pagoda-roofed drive-in exterior referencing the Kirby's Pig Stand origin architecture specifically, rather than the more familiar canopy-and-stalls form

## Ice House Beer Sign

**What it is.** The Texas 'icehouse' as a building and business type. Originally literal ice-storage and ice-vending depots (pre-refrigeration), Texas ice merchants diversified into selling groceries and cold beer, becoming early convenience stores and gathering places -- the same lineage that produced the Southland Ice Company / Tote'm / 7-Eleven chain (see entry above). Separately, and specifically in San Antonio and the Texas Hill Country down to the Mexican border, 'icehouse' became a colloquial term for an establishment deriving most of its income from cold beer sales, a pattern one source attributes to the region's Catholicism, its deeper-rooted Mexican culture, and German immigrant influence.

**Why it lands.** The icehouse-as-beer-bar is a specifically South Texas/Hill Country social institution, architecturally and culturally distinct from an enclosed Northern-style bar -- open-sided, unhurried, a domino table under a bare bulb, screen walls instead of solid ones, built for heat rather than against it.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** No brand-generic protection applies to the building type itself; only a specific real icehouse business's actual name or sign, or whatever specific beer-brand signage (Lone Star, Shiner, Pearl) might hang inside or on it, carries protection.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** The open-air icehouse building type itself: a simple wood or concrete-block structure with one or more walls replaced by screen mesh or left fully open, garage-style roll-up door openings, a shaded porch or patio extension with picnic tables, a walk-up service window instead of an enclosed dining room, a bare-bulb or string-light ceiling. This screen-walled, beer-forward, open-air vernacular is a real, documented regional building type belonging to the culture rather than to any single business, fully free to draw undecorated.

**Geometry.** UNCERTAIN on any specific verified icehouse building dimensions -- not verified this session. A generic low, single-story, open-sided structure at roughly barn- or carport-scale is a safe familiar approximation.

**Palette.** UNCERTAIN on any specific paint colors -- not verified this session. Generic vernacular materials read safely as unpainted or whitewashed concrete block, weathered wood, and rusted corrugated tin roofing.

**Era markers.** The lineage split itself is the useful era marker: a pre-refrigeration literal ice-storage depot (thick-walled, sawdust-insulated, minimal windows) is an earlier variant, while the diversified, open, screened, social 'icehouse' is the later and visually richer variant for this project's purposes.

**Regional.** Strongest specifically in 'San Antonio and the Texas Hill Country down to the Mexican border' per source; the broader ice-then-convenience-store lineage traces separately to Dallas via the Southland Ice Company.

**Variants.** an open screen-walled patio with a domino table under string lights, the core social-scene beat a walk-up beer window at a low concrete-block building a bare, unbranded pre-refrigeration ice-depot exterior, an earlier and rarer variant a shaded porch extension with picnic tables and an implied unbranded beer-sign glow (cross-reference the texas_beer_sign entry for the actual glowing sign object)

**Scene role.** A South Texas/Hill Country social-gathering establishing beat, pairing directly with the texas_beer_sign and shiner entries for a layered night-scene composition.

## Neon Motel Cafe Sign

**What it is.** The mid-century roadside neon sign as a Texas highway-corridor form, evidenced by two documented Texas examples. First, the Tower Station and U-Drop Inn in Shamrock, Texas (built 1936, designed by John Nunn and architect J.C. Berry): a Conoco gas station combined with a cafe, its twin flared, stepped-concrete Art Deco towers clad in green-and-gold glazed terra-cotta tile, beige stucco walls with dark green accents, neon tube lighting outlining the towers, a metal 'tulip' ornament atop the main tower, block-capital 'CONOCO' signage on the western tower, restored 2000-2003 for 1.7 million dollars and now a museum and visitor center. Second, the Big Texan Steak Ranch in Amarillo (established 1960 on Route 66, relocated to an Interstate 40 frontage road in 1970): a building painted bright yellow with blue trim, and a towering illuminated sign featuring a cowboy figure with his hat pushed back, built to evoke an Old West feeling, famous for its 72-ounce steak challenge and extensive West Texas billboard advertising.

**Why it lands.** These signs were built to be read by a driver deciding, in about two seconds at highway speed, whether to take the next exit -- glowing tube outlines and oversized figures visible for miles across flat Panhandle terrain, functioning as both advertisement and de facto highway landmark.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** The specific historic U-Drop Inn/Tower Conoco building and its specific ornament (the CONOCO brand mark itself remains a live trademark), and the Big Texan's specific sign design, wordmark, and cowboy-figure artwork.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** A flared, stepped Art Deco TOWER silhouette (a vertical element rising above a flat-roofed rectangular building, capped with a simple ornamental finial) clad in glazed tile is a broader 1930s Art Deco commercial-architecture convention, not owned by any one gas station. A neon TUBE-OUTLINE technique -- glowing colored glass tubing following a building's edges or a sign's letterforms -- is a generic 1920s-60s sign-making technology (nearly 2,000 small neon-sign shops existed nationally by 1940 per source), freely usable to outline any invented building or lettering. A large illuminated cowboy-figure silhouette (hat pushed back, a Western stance) is a generic Western-iconography convention, distinct from the Big Texan's own specific figure design, as long as that specific artwork is not copied. A generic cursive-script sign-word rendered in tube-light style for an invented business name is a free generic period lettering style.

**Geometry.** The U-Drop Inn's twin towers are described as flared, stepped, cast-concrete forms rising above a flat-roofed rectangular main building. No verified dimensions for either building were found this session (UNCERTAIN); a tower reading roughly one-and-a-half to two times the main building's wall height is offered here only as a compositional approximation for drawing purposes, not a sourced measurement.

**Palette.** The U-Drop Inn is specifically documented as green-and-gold glazed terra-cotta towers with beige stucco walls and dark green trim accents. The Big Texan is specifically documented as bright yellow with blue trim. Treat both combinations as tied to their specific real sites; vary the combination (for example blue-and-cream or maroon-and-cream tile for a generic tower-cafe, or a different two-color building-and-trim pairing for a generic cowboy-sign roadhouse) to draw 'the same genre' without copying either site. Neon tube color itself is generically available across a wide range: early neon glowed reddish-orange only, until a 1926 phosphor-coating innovation opened up nearly 100 available colors by the 1960s, so any tube color is period-plausible depending on the decade depicted.

**Era markers.** Reddish-orange-only neon (pre-1926 phosphor-coating innovation) versus a full color range (1926 onward, maturing through the 1960s) is a genuine, sourced technical era-tell. Route 66 itself was decommissioned in 1984 per source, so a neglected, powered-down building state versus a fresh 1930s-50s heyday state offers a 'then versus abandoned versus restored' storytelling arc, directly evidenced by the U-Drop Inn's own documented decline-then-2000-2003-restoration history.

**Regional.** Texas Panhandle/Route 66 corridor specifically for both sourced examples (Shamrock and Amarillo); the broader neon-motel-and-cafe form is a national mid-century phenomenon with a concentrated, well-preserved Texas expression along old Route 66.

**Variants.** a flared Art Deco tower glowing with neon tube outlines at dusk, the signature establishing beat a large illuminated cowboy-figure sign against a night sky, a roadside-icon beat a daytime shot emphasizing tile and stucco color and geometric massing without the glow, an architecture-study beat a faded, powered-down version of the same tower under daytime sun, referencing the pre-restoration decline period for a bygone-era mood

**Scene role.** A signature nighttime highway-landmark beat, strong as a hero image in a wide establishing shot; pairs with the drive_in_menu_board and texas_beer_sign entries for a layered 'highway strip after dark' sequence.

## Roadside Barn Advertising

**What it is.** Painted-barn advertising as a genre, most thoroughly documented via the Mail Pouch Tobacco program (1891-1992; roughly 20,000 barns across 22 states at its 1960s peak). Barns were typically painted black or red with yellow or white capital lettering reading CHEW MAIL POUCH TOBACCO -- TREAT YOURSELF TO THE BEST, some with a thin vertical blue border, on one or two sides depending on road visibility. Owners were paid modestly (one to two dollars a year) but were primarily compensated with a free repaint every few years that preserved the wood. Painter Harley Warrick alone reportedly painted roughly 20,000 barns after WWII. The program was specifically exempted from removal under the Highway Beautification Act of 1965 as a recognized historic-landmark category, and ended in 1992. The sourced state list for this specific campaign does NOT include Texas.

**Why it lands.** Even without a verified Texas-specific painted-tobacco-barn campaign, the underlying visual grammar -- huge hand-painted capital letters wrapped across the broad side of a barn, meant to be read once at highway speed -- is instantly legible as old rural roadside advertising, and is a safe, honest generic form for this project as long as it is not presented as a real historic Texas Mail Pouch barn, since none is verified to have existed.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** The actual 'Mail Pouch' wordmark and its specific slogan copy and color scheme belong to that brand's history and should not be reproduced verbatim even in a pastiche context; current legal status of the mark was not verified this session (UNCERTAIN).

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** The barn-as-billboard compositional convention itself: a large gable-roofed barn's broad side used as a flat ad surface, huge blocky hand-painted capital letters (an invented product or slogan) spanning most of the wall's width, in a high-contrast two-color scheme, positioned to face a nearby road. This entire convention -- a barn wall functioning as a sign -- is generic and safe to draw with invented text and branding.

**Geometry.** Letters sized to be legible from a moving car at highway distance are, by the genre's own logic, very large relative to a person -- multi-foot-tall capitals spanning most of a barn's gable-end width is a safe general proportion. No specific verified letter-height dimension was found this session (UNCERTAIN).

**Palette.** Mail Pouch specifically used black-or-red barns with yellow-or-white lettering and an optional thin blue vertical border -- treat this exact combination as tied to that historic brand. A generic invented-text barn ad should use a different high-contrast pairing (dark green with cream lettering, or barn-red with plain white lettering and no blue border) to stay clearly generic while keeping the same high-legibility logic.

**Era markers.** Weathered, sun-bleached lettering with visible brush-stroke texture and slightly irregular hand-painted letterforms reads as pre-1990s. The painted-barn-ad practice generally is understood as a pre-interstate or early-interstate-era roadside form, tapering off as regulated billboard advertising and highway-beautification-era attitudes changed the roadside landscape; the Mail Pouch program's own 1992 end date is a useful anchor, even though it is not itself a Texas date.

**Regional.** Mail Pouch's own documented footprint spans Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Maryland, Michigan, Ohio, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, New York, South Carolina, Tennessee, Wisconsin, West Virginia, Connecticut, and California -- explicitly NOT Texas per the verified source. Use this entry's generic vernacular in a Texas scene only as an unbranded, invented-text homage to the broader genre, not as a claim that this specific campaign painted Texas barns.

**Variants.** a weathered barn gable-end with large invented capital lettering, the core generic beat a barn photographed obliquely from a passing-car point of view, a motion/roadside-glimpse beat a faded, partially peeling version of the same for an aged mood a fresh-paint version referencing the real program's repaint-as-payment arrangement, usable generically without the specific brand

**Scene role.** A rural-highway driving-montage beat; use only as an unbranded generic homage given the verified absence of a real Texas precedent for this specific historic campaign.

## County Fair Midway

**Why it lands.** The county-fair midway is a dense sensory environment more than a single sign -- competing colors and bulb-chases at every booth, layered amplified sound, food-stand steam and neon together -- and Texas has its own outsized, verified superlative version of this at the State Fair (Big Tex, the Texas Star wheel) that reads as distinctly Texan rather than generic Americana.

## Co Op Cap

**What it is.** The 'gimme cap' or seed cap as agricultural promotional merchandise: originating in the 1970s as a giveaway from feed and farm-supply companies to farmers, truck drivers, and rural workers, combining a stiff structured foam front panel (built for an embroidered patch or logo) with a breathable plastic mesh back, a curved bill, an adjustable snap or hook-and-loop rear closure, and a six-triangular-panel crown meeting at a covered top button. The style underwent an early-2000s ironic fashion revival among suburban youth, valued specifically for its working-class connotations.

**Why it lands.** The most literally wearable piece of Texas commercial-sign culture -- a small piece of the same branded-giveaway economy that painted grain elevators and feed-store walls, scaled down to a human head and worn without irony for decades by the same farmers and ranchers who bought the feed in the first place, before its 2000s revival flipped the meaning to costume.

**PROTECTED, never draw.** Any specific real company's actual embroidered logo or patch on a cap. No specific current cap-patch artwork from any named brand was independently verified this session; treat any such patch as off-limits to reproduce and use an invented mark instead.

**DRAWABLE vernacular.** The cap construction itself is entirely generic and unownable: the foam-front/mesh-back structural combination, the six-panel crown with a top button, the curved bill, and the snap-or-hook-and-loop rear closure are generic garment-construction conventions that predate and outlive any single company's use of them. A blank cap, or a cap bearing an entirely invented patch mark, is fully free to draw in complete constructional detail.

**Geometry.** Six triangular fabric panels meeting at a covered top button is the confirmed generic crown construction. The front panel sits noticeably taller and stiffer than a typical softer baseball cap because of its internal foam structure, a confirmed relative-proportion detail rather than an exact measurement. UNCERTAIN on any precise dimensions -- not verified this session.

**Palette.** A two-tone convention (one color for the structured foam front, often a different color or material for the mesh back) is a widely familiar generic construction choice rather than any brand's mark; any color combination is safe for an invented cap.

**Era markers.** The foam-front/mesh-back structured cap is the specific 1970s-originating agricultural-giveaway form. Its later 2000s hipster/streetwear revival is a different cultural context for the same object -- for pure rural-commercial nostalgia rather than 2000s irony, stage the cap in an unambiguously workwear context (dirt, sweat stain, tucked in a back pocket) rather than a fashion-forward one.

**Regional.** National in origin as a promotional-merchandise form, but ubiquitous specifically among Texas farmers, ranchers, and feed-store customers as default headwear, worn alongside the feed-store and grain-elevator signage covered above.

**Variants.** a blank or invented-patch cap in isolation, a prop/detail beat a cap on a generic farmer or rancher figure's head, brim shading the eyes, at a feed store or fairground a row of caps on a store display rack, a generic retail-merchandising beat with invented patches only a sweat-and-dust-worn cap tucked in a back pocket, a texture/character beat

**Scene role.** A small, mobile character-costuming prop, useful anywhere a generic Texas rural figure needs a single, instantly legible headwear cue.
