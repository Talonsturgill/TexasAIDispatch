# Landmarks — the built and natural Texas a viewer names on sight

The most efficient way to place a frame in Texas is a landmark a viewer already knows. This is the
research on the thirty that carry the most recognition, kept in full because the reasoning is the
value: the tell that makes it read, the geometry, the era markers, the regional note, and the
trademark flag where one exists. Draw the FORM, never the MARK. Read `CULTURE.md` first for the
ones that are shrines rather than sights.

Marks: **[MARK]** a live trademark or trade dress sits on or around it, so draw the structure and
omit the mark. Everything else is a public silhouette or an architectural form and is drawable.
Nothing here needs a logo to be recognized.

These are not built as artifacts yet. This file is the brief for when a landmark module is written,
and the durable home for research that otherwise lived only in a scratch file.

## Alamo  [MARK]

**What it is.** The chapel and long barrack of Mission San Antonio de Valero, a Spanish mission secularized in 1793 and converted into a fort, where in 1836 a garrison of Texian and Tejano defenders held out for thirteen days against General Santa Anna's army before being overrun. It is the most reproduced building outline in the state, treated as a civic-religious shrine rather than a normal historic site.

**Why it lands.** It carries almost sacred civic weight (Remember the Alamo), and every Texan schoolchild visits it. The thing outsiders get consistently wrong is twofold: they draw it as a lone mission on an open plain, when it actually sits in the middle of dense modern downtown San Antonio, wedged between hotel towers, a wax museum and an IMAX; and they draw the famous scalloped roofline on an 1836 battle scene, when that roofline was added around 1850, fourteen years after the battle, by the US Army.

**Geometry.** The signature shape is the campanulate (bell shaped, scrolled) parapet crowning the facade, an outline of stacked curves rising to a peak, added circa 1850, not present during the battle. Below it, a single story chapel front with two low, flat topped stubs where bell towers and a dome were planned in the 1750s but never finished, so the roofline reads asymmetric and blunt rather than a classic domed mission. Walls are four foot thick limestone. The doorway has a carved stone surround (pilasters, Spanish Baroque frontier detailing) with an arched window directly above it and a niche on each side (exact statue-fill status UNCERTAIN, verify against current photos before finalizing niche contents). The original full mission compound measured about 480 by 160 feet; today only the chapel and the Long Barrack (a plain two story flat roofed convento building) survive, fronting a paved plaza. Silhouette rule: the scalloped hump against the sky is the entire identity of the shape. Draw the facade without it and it reads as a generic Spanish mission, not the Alamo.

**Palette.** Warm buff to tan limestone, weathered grey white in the recessed carving, deep shadow in the doorway surround. Modern plaza shows green lawn, a flagpole, and the Texas flag. No original paint scheme to preserve, the stone itself is the material.

**Era markers.** Pre-1850 or in-battle depictions must NOT show the scalloped parapet (period accounts and the Alamo Trust's own research indicate a flatter coping line at the time of the fight). Post-1850 photographs and all modern depictions show the hump. Any honest present-day depiction should include the downtown context (hotel towers, street level crowds) rather than isolating the building on open ground.

**Regional.** Downtown San Antonio, Alamo Plaza. Recognized by essentially all Texans and a large majority of Americans as the state's founding symbol.

**Variants.** Straight on three quarter hero facade, tightly cropped to isolate the building Wide honest shot showing the chapel dwarfed by surrounding hotel towers and tourist crowds Dusk or uplit silhouette with the scalloped parapet backlit against a darkening sky Detail crop of the carved doorway surround and flanking niches

**Scene role.** Default cold open or establishing shot for any Texas identity story. Used for tourism, preservation, or Texas history beats. A corrective wide shot (showing the downtown context) is the strongest way to signal the piece is being careful and accurate rather than reaching for the cliche.

**Flag.** Not a corporate trademark concern for the building's public silhouette (state stewardship via the Alamo Trust), but the Alamo Trust's specific crest, wordmark and merchandise logos are managed marks. Draw the building, never their crest. Also a solemn battle-death site: keep any battle-era depiction respectful and non-graphic, no combat violence.

## Mission San Jose

**What it is.** Mission San Jose y San Miguel de Aguayo, founded 1720, called the Queen of the Missions, the largest and most architecturally ornate of the San Antonio missions and still an active Catholic parish today.

**Why it lands.** It is the mission postcard image, with the famous Rose Window and a completed bell tower and dome the Alamo's chapel never got. Outsiders routinely conflate every San Antonio mission with the Alamo and have often never heard of it, even though it is architecturally the grander building.

**Geometry.** Limestone church with an elaborately carved Baroque facade, figures stacked vertically above the door: a cross at the top, then Saint Joseph holding the infant Jesus, then Saints Dominic and Francis, then Our Lady of Guadalupe, then Saint Joachim and Saint Anne with the infant Mary. A single completed bell tower and dome combination sits beside the facade, unlike the Alamo's unfinished stubs. The Rose Window is a small, ornate, deeply carved round window on the south sacristy wall, carved 1775, traditionally attributed to sculptor Pedro Huizar. A full walled quadrangle compound survives, with small rooms built directly into the outer wall (housing for roughly 350 people), plus a restored granary building with a distinctive barrel vaulted stone roof and buttressed arches, and a stone grist mill.

**Palette.** Warm gold tan limestone, deep carved shadow in the Baroque figures, traces of polychrome in restored sections, arid Hill Country scrub inside and around the compound walls.

**Era markers.** Pre-1930s depictions should show it in partial ruin, roof collapsed, facade weathering, no dome. The 1930s federal restoration rebuilt the roof and dome. It remains an active parish, so a contemporary scene may include worshippers or a mass in progress.

**Regional.** Southeast of downtown San Antonio, on the river's Mission Reach, part of San Antonio Missions National Historical Park and the 2015 UNESCO World Heritage designation shared with the Alamo and the other missions. Well known within Texas, a footnote for most outsiders next to the Alamo.

**Variants.** Full facade hero shot showing the stacked saint niches and completed tower Rose Window macro detail as a standalone graphic element Wide shot of the walled quadrangle with granary and mill visible Golden hour raking light across the carved stonework to emphasize relief depth

**Scene role.** Deeper Texas history beats, Tejano and Hispanic heritage stories, architecture segments, and as a contrast beat against the Alamo's plainer, unfinished chapel.

**Flag.** None beyond general respect due to it being an active house of worship; avoid staging that would read as mocking or trivializing an operating parish.

## San Antonio Missions Trail

**What it is.** Mission Concepcion (1755, the oldest unrestored stone church in the United States), Mission San Juan Capistrano (established 1731) and Mission San Francisco de la Espada (founded 1690, relocated to this site 1731), three working Spanish colonial churches strung along the river south of downtown San Antonio, forming San Antonio Missions National Historical Park together with Mission San Jose and the Alamo's original mission identity.

**Why it lands.** San Antonians experience the missions as a set, connected today by the Mission Reach hike and bike trail along the river. Outsiders usually know only the Alamo and are surprised four more working colonial churches exist a few miles south of it.

**Geometry.** Concepcion: twin bell towers flanking a plain squared facade, cruciform floor plan, four foot thick limestone walls, faded frescoes still visible on interior and some exterior plaster surfaces, the largest surviving concentration of Spanish colonial frescoes in the United States. San Juan: a single espadana, a flat vertical bell wall pierced with arched openings rather than a full tower, small and plain, with a Romanesque arched gate at the compound entrance. Espada: also a simple espadana bell wall, asymmetric and modest, notable for a slightly crooked arch over its door, a widely loved imperfection rather than a flaw to correct in a drawing.

**Palette.** Pale tan to grey limestone, largely unplastered and raw in patches, arid scrub and mesquite around the compounds, the acequia (irrigation ditch) running nearby as a thin green lined water channel.

**Era markers.** No strong era markers beyond general colonial character versus modern National Park Service trail signage, bike path pavement, and interpretive markers.

**Regional.** San Antonio's Mission Reach, a National Park Service unit and part of the 2015 UNESCO World Heritage Site. Well known to San Antonians, a lesser known footnote to most other Texans and outsiders.

**Variants.** Concepcion's twin towers straight on against open sky San Juan's plain espadana wall silhouette Espada's crooked door arch as a charming close detail Wide river trail establishing shot with a mission in the distance and cyclists or the acequia in the foreground

**Scene role.** Quick cut montage beat (more than just the Alamo), heritage or river restoration B-roll texture, rarely a hero shot on its own.

**Flag.** None beyond general respect, all three remain active or semi-active houses of worship.

## Espada Aqueduct

**What it is.** A two arch Spanish colonial stone aqueduct built between 1731 and 1745, carrying the Espada acequia (irrigation ditch) over Piedras Creek, the oldest Spanish aqueduct still in operation in the United States.

**Why it lands.** It is nearly 300 years old and still does its original job, carrying real water today. That functioning-infrastructure fact is the specific pride point, distinct from the missions' religious architecture nearby.

**Geometry.** Two stone arches, one 12 feet in diameter and one 16.5 feet in diameter, plus one central support pier, spanning a total of 195 feet including the abutments at each end. Stands 15.5 feet high at the creek crossing. The water channel riding on top is 4 feet wide and 4 feet deep with 1.5 foot thick retaining walls. Profile is low and horizontal, a squat double arched bridge rather than a tall dramatic span, built of rough coursed limestone masonry.

**Palette.** Rough tan to grey limestone, green creek vegetation below, and, crucially, a visible thin ribbon of flowing water along the top channel, the detail that distinguishes it from an ordinary stone bridge.

**Era markers.** Colonial construction with no major stylistic changes since; a modern National Park Service trail and small interpretive signage mark it as a contemporary heritage site.

**Regional.** South of Mission Espada on San Antonio's Mission Reach. A proud niche landmark, best known to San Antonians, historians and infrastructure enthusiasts rather than a mainstream tourist stop.

**Variants.** Side profile showing both arches and the central pier Close detail on the top water channel with visible flowing water Low angle from the creek bed looking up through an arch Wide shot with the acequia trail and cyclists or hikers for scale

**Scene role.** Hidden gem or did-you-know beat, engineering and infrastructure angle, brief connective B-roll between larger San Antonio segments.

**Flag.** None.

## San Antonio Riverwalk  [MARK]

**What it is.** A pedestrian walkway sunken about 20 feet below street level along a horseshoe bend of the San Antonio River through downtown, designed by architect Robert Hugman beginning 1929 as flood control paired with tourism, opened 1941.

**Why it lands.** It is downtown San Antonio's entire identity and economy, built on Hugman's deliberate fantasy blend of Spain and Venice. Outsiders often assume it is a natural river feature; it is in fact a heavily engineered flood control and tourism project, built partly in response to the deadly 1921 flood that nearly drained support for the river itself.

**Geometry.** Two level city: ordinary street grid above, sunken pedestrian level below. Stone paved walkways line both riverbanks behind rusticated limestone retaining walls. Roughly twenty arched pedestrian bridges in simple round arch spans, built of native limestone. A canopy of mature bald cypress and other trees shades the water. Flat bottomed tour barges with striped awnings are the signature moving element on the water itself, low profile, open sided, poled or motored slowly along the bend.

**Palette.** Warm terracotta and stucco building facades along the walk (Spanish Colonial Revival and Mediterranean influences), deep green cypress and fern understory, warm string lighting at night, brown green river water.

**Era markers.** 1920s to 1940s WPA era stonework is the historic core (the horseshoe bend downtown). Later Museum Reach (north) and Mission Reach (south) extensions, built in the 2000s and 2010s, read more naturalized and less urban, so the choice of stretch signals the era and mood of the scene.

**Regional.** Downtown San Antonio. One of the most visited attractions in Texas, near universal recognition statewide.

**Variants.** Classic tour barge eye level shot passing under a stone arch bridge Elevated angle showing the horseshoe bend and riverside cafe terraces Night shot with string lights reflected on the water The tour barge itself, isolated, as a small recurring vehicle icon

**Scene role.** Default downtown San Antonio establishing shot, tourism and economy stories, romantic or leisure beats, nightlife texture.

**Flag.** None for the public walkway itself; individual restaurants and shops along it carry their own separate commercial signage, avoid reproducing any specific business's branding.

## Texas State Capitol

**What it is.** The seat of the Texas Legislature and the governor's offices in Austin, completed 1888, built of sunset red granite in Renaissance Revival style, the largest state capitol by floor area and, at 302.64 feet, taller than the United States Capitol in Washington.

**Why it lands.** Bigger than the national one is a specific, factual, constantly repeated point of state pride. Outsiders tend to picture state capitols as interchangeable grey or white domes and miss that the Texas Capitol's pink toned granite is genuinely unusual among them.

**Geometry.** A central four story block topped by a tall dome rising from a colonnaded drum, flanked by symmetrical three story wings extending east and west. A deep front portico with classical columns and a pediment faces south down Congress Avenue. Footprint is 2.25 acres, with 360,000 square feet of floor space, nearly 400 rooms and more than 900 windows. The building is capped by the Goddess of Liberty, a female figure holding a star aloft in one hand and a sword in the other (current casting is aluminum, roughly 3,000 pounds, installed by helicopter in 1986, replacing an earlier zinc figure). Silhouette rule: dome on colonnaded drum on a symmetrical winged block, topped by the star bearing figure, rendered in a visibly warmer pink toned stone than most other state capitols.

**Palette.** Sunset red (pink) granite from Granite Mountain near Marble Falls. Green capitol grounds lawn and live oak trees surround it. Exact dome cladding accent color beyond the granite is UNCERTAIN, verify against current photography before rendering fine dome detail.

**Era markers.** Built 1882 to 1888, cornerstone laid 1885, opened 1888, fixed dates with no major exterior redesign since. The 1986 aluminum Goddess of Liberty versus the original zinc figure is the one dateable variant if the statue is shown in close detail.

**Regional.** Austin. Recognized statewide as the literal seat of Texas government, near universal recognition.

**Variants.** South facade hero shot with portico, columns and dome Dome and Goddess of Liberty isolated against open sky Wide shot down Congress Avenue framing the dome at the street's vanishing point Interior rotunda looking straight up, if an interior beat is needed

**Scene role.** Default state government or state capital establishing shot, civic pride beats, and a ready made contrast device for any state pride statistic ('taller than the other one').

**Flag.** None, a public government building.

## UT Tower

**What it is.** The Main Building tower at the University of Texas at Austin, 307 feet tall with 27 floors, completed 1937 as the centerpiece of Paul Cret's Beaux-Arts campus plan, famous today for its color coded celebratory lighting and, somberly, as the site of the 1966 mass shooting.

**Why it lands.** The tower's lit up orange tonight is specific campus shorthand every UT alum recognizes instantly, for wins, graduation and other milestones. Outsiders often see only a generic campus building and miss its real architectural pedigree, and the 1966 tragedy is either omitted from casual tourism material entirely or, in the opposite failure, sensationalized.

**Geometry.** A slender square plan tower rising from a lower, symmetrical Beaux-Arts base building with ground level colonnades and arcades. Clock faces sit on all four sides near the top. A public observation deck near the summit (28th floor, reached from the 27th) is ringed by an open arcaded gallery, capped by a small stepped cupola or lantern at the very top. Silhouette rule: the tower is slim and vertical with a distinctly tiered crown, base block, plain shaft, clock stage, open colonnade ring, small lantern cap, never a flat topped single block. It is depicted either fully dark, fully burnt orange lit, or lit with a numeral for a championship.

**Palette.** Cream to buff limestone cladding (exact stone mix UNCERTAIN, verify before close texture rendering). Burnt orange floodlighting at night for celebrations, UT's specific school color, distinct from a generic Halloween orange. Otherwise stone colored and unlit by day.

**Era markers.** Nothing before 1937. The 1966 shooting closed the observation deck for decades; it later reopened with safety barriers. A 2015 relamping moved the tower to LED lighting, a technical change not usually visible in a drawing.

**Regional.** Austin, UT campus. Recognized statewide and, through college sports, nationally as the Longhorns' defining landmark.

**Variants.** Full tower dark against a blue sky daytime campus shot Tower lit burnt orange at night for a win or graduation Tower lit with a numeral for a championship Close crop on the clock stage and open colonnade gallery

**Scene role.** Campus and education beats, football Saturday texture, state pride night skyline shots. Reserve tragedy-anniversary use only for stories specifically and respectfully about that history.

**Flag.** TRAGEDY SITE, handle with care. The 1966 shooting killed sixteen people. Never depict a rifle, a shooter figure, or a sniper's eye view from the deck. Reference the history only architecturally (the tower itself, the campus memorial garden) and only when the story is specifically about that history, never as ambient decoration.

## Congress Avenue Bat Bridge

**What it is.** The Ann W. Richards Congress Avenue Bridge in downtown Austin, a 1910 concrete arch bridge whose expansion joint crevices house the largest urban bat colony in North America, up to 1.5 million Mexican free tailed bats each summer.

**Why it lands.** It is a nightly free wildlife spectacle in the middle of a capital city, a specific point of Austin pride treated as a beloved civic ritual, complete with cheering crowds and boat tours, rather than a spooky motif. Outsiders default to a Halloween association the locals do not share.

**Geometry.** A long, low concrete arch bridge, 945.9 feet long and 60 feet wide, spanning Lady Bird Lake with multiple shallow arches along its length. At dusk, bats emerge as a dense streaming ribbon pouring from beneath the bridge deck, banking low over the water toward the east. Silhouette rule: the landmark is the ribbon of bats streaming from a plain flat bridge, not the bridge alone, which is an ordinary municipal arch bridge on its own. Omitting the bat plume loses the landmark entirely.

**Palette.** Pale grey to tan concrete bridge. Warm orange pink dusk sky behind the emerging cloud. Dark speckled silhouette for the bats themselves. Dark water below with light reflections, a generic downtown Austin skyline visible behind if wanted.

**Era markers.** The colony's season (roughly March through October, peak numbers midsummer) is a seasonal marker rather than an architectural one. The bridge's own facade has not changed meaningfully since 1910.

**Regional.** Downtown Austin, over Lady Bird Lake. Extremely well known statewide and to Austin visitors, moderately known nationally.

**Variants.** Wide dusk shot of the full bat emergence plume against sunset Close crop of bats streaming from a single bridge crevice Crowd or kayak eye view looking up into the cloud The bridge alone in daylight, bat free, as a before beat

**Scene role.** Nightlife and nature-in-the-city beats, default Austin establishing shot, seasonal timely hook (the bats' spring return is a recurring local news moment), a quirky charming button on a segment.

**Flag.** None.

## Reunion Tower  [MARK]

**What it is.** A 561 foot observation tower in downtown Dallas, completed 1978 beside the Hyatt Regency and the former Reunion Arena, topped by a lit geodesic sphere that functions as the city's default night skyline signature.

**Why it lands.** It is Dallas's answer to Seattle's Space Needle, a ball that changes color for holidays, sports wins and civic moments, used as instant visual shorthand for this is Dallas. Outsiders sometimes dismiss it as a mere tourist restaurant and miss that it is a genuine structural engineering piece built from four poured concrete shafts.

**Geometry.** A tall slender cylindrical central concrete shaft carrying stairs and mechanical systems, joined by three additional rectangular elevator shafts, widening slightly where the observation floors sit partway up. Capped by an open lattice geodesic sphere, aluminum struts with lights at the 259 (originally) or, after a 2026 upgrade, 8,547 strut intersection points, reading as a glowing dotted ball at night. Silhouette rule: thin vertical shaft plus a faceted, lattice (never smooth) ball on top is the entire shape.

**Palette.** Grey to white concrete shaft by day, dark grey lattice ball by day. At night the ball becomes the whole point, capable of solid colors, patterns or full animation across its RGB LED points, set against a dark skyline.

**Era markers.** Built 1978. Original colored lighting (259 points) installed 2011 was already an upgrade from plain white. A 2026 relighting expanded to 8,547 individually addressable points, so a current depiction can show much richer color and animation than the simpler colored ball look of the 2010s.

**Regional.** Downtown Dallas. The single most recognizable Dallas skyline element statewide and nationally.

**Variants.** Full tower silhouette against a dusk skyline The geodesic ball alone, lit, as an isolated icon Wide skyline shot with the tower as the tallest, brightest element among generic downtown high rises Daytime shot showing the plain concrete structure without lighting drama

**Scene role.** Default this-is-Dallas establishing or closing shot, night skyline texture, celebratory color change beats, general backdrop for any DFW based story.

**Flag.** None, though it sits beside a Hyatt hotel; draw the tower structure only, no hotel branding.

## Dallas Pegasus Sign  [MARK]

**What it is.** A rooftop neon sign of a rearing, winged red horse atop the Magnolia Building (1922) in downtown Dallas, erected 1934 as the trademark of Magnolia Petroleum Company, found beyond repair and entirely rebuilt from scratch in 1999 to 2000.

**Why it lands.** For decades this was the tallest, brightest object in the Dallas skyline and the de facto city symbol before any modern skyscraper existed, so older Dallasites carry real nostalgic attachment to it. Outsiders often see a generic decorative winged horse and do not realize it began life as, and functionally still is, an oil company's trademark.

**Geometry.** A rearing, leaping horse shown in profile, large sweeping wings extended back and up, mounted on a tall rooftop pylon above the Magnolia Building's cornice. It is a LINE figure, outlined entirely in neon tubing rather than rendered as a solid mass, designed to read as a glowing contour drawing at night. It rotates slowly. Silhouette rule: draw it as luminous contour lines on a dark ground, never as a filled solid shape, that distinction is what makes it read as a sign rather than a statue.

**Palette.** Red neon outline against black night sky (its nickname is literally the Flying Red Horse). The Beaux-Arts building beneath it reads cream to white terra cotta by day.

**Era markers.** Original 1934 sign rotated until a 1974 mechanical failure, then sat static for decades. The current sign is an entire 1999 to 2000 recreation, relit and re-rotating from January 1, 2000. A vintage postcard depiction and a modern skyline depiction both show valid but physically different signs.

**Regional.** Downtown Dallas. Iconic statewide as Texas's oldest and, at the time, largest illuminated sign.

**Variants.** Night shot, red neon horse glowing atop the building silhouette Daytime shot showing the sign as a dark steel and tube frame against blue sky Close crop of just the horse in profile, rearing, wings raised Wide period skyline shot from the 1930s through 1950s where the Pegasus is the tallest lit object in frame, useful for nostalgia beats

**Scene role.** Nostalgia and Dallas-origin-story beats, night skyline texture, period pieces set before the modern skyline existed.

**Flag.** LIVE TRADEMARK. This is functionally Mobil and ExxonMobil's flying horse corporate mark (Magnolia Petroleum merged into Mobil in 1959), even though the physical sign is now civic heritage. Draw the winged horse landmark for atmosphere and always caption it as the specific Dallas landmark, never imply current commercial sponsorship or use it as a generic reusable brand mark.

## Dealey Plaza Depository

**What it is.** The former Texas School Book Depository building (built 1901, rebuilt 1902 to 1903) at 411 Elm Street in Dallas, overlooking Dealey Plaza, from a sixth floor corner window of which Lee Harvey Oswald shot and killed President John F. Kennedy on November 22, 1963. The building now houses Dallas County offices and the respected Sixth Floor Museum.

**Why it lands.** It is one of the most visited historic sites in Texas precisely because it is a real, still unresolved national trauma. A Texan raises it with gravity, never casually. The site, the museum, the two small white X marks painted on Elm Street marking the approximate shot locations, the grassy rise to the north, is treated as a place of memory and continuing historical inquiry, not a novelty stop.

**Geometry.** A plain seven to eight story, early 1900s brick industrial and commercial building, Romanesque Revival detailing, arched window heads on the upper floor, an otherwise unremarkable rectangular block distinguished only by its corner position over the plaza and the sixth floor southeast corner window. Dealey Plaza itself is a shallow grassy park sloping down from the intersecting streets toward a triple underpass, with white stone pergolas at its west end and a turfed rise (the grassy knoll) along the north side of Elm Street.

**Palette.** Dark red brown brick, white painted window trim, green plaza lawn, white stone colonnade structures. If the street itself is shown, the two small white roadway X marks are a specific, widely documented, factual detail rather than embellishment.

**Era markers.** The building's exterior is essentially unchanged since the 1960s. County administration signage and the museum's presence (opened 1989) are the only dateable modern additions.

**Regional.** Downtown Dallas. Universally known nationally and internationally, not only within Texas.

**Variants.** Wide daylight establishing shot of the building over the plaza, calm documentary register The plaza's white pergola and colonnade alone as a quieter architectural stand in The building facade alone, cropped to avoid making the window the visual subject AVOID any first person window viewpoint or crosshair style framing, do not create this variant

**Scene role.** Only for explicit, respectful historical or anniversary journalism about the assassination itself. Not a general purpose Dallas establishing shot.

**Flag.** TRAGEDY SITE, handle with extreme care. A sitting president's assassination, with profound ongoing national grief and a culturally loaded conspiracy-theory landscape. Depict only the building and plaza, exterior, daylight, documentary register, no reenactment of the motorcade or the shooting, no gun barrel or crosshair viewpoint from the window, no dramatic zoom framing the window as a reveal. If a story is not specifically and respectfully about this history, skip the site entirely rather than reuse it as generic old-Dallas texture. When used, caption plainly and factually, never speculate on theories.

## Arlington Football Stadium  [MARK]

**What it is.** The Dallas Cowboys' home stadium in Arlington, opened 2009, designed by HKS with lead designer Bryan Trubey, replacing the team's previous stadium, which was itself famous for its open roof over the field. Sponsor naming rights on the building have already changed since it opened, which is the reason to describe it by its architecture rather than by whichever corporate name is current.

**Why it lands.** The twin steel arches and huge retractable roof are a genuine engineering spectacle, continuing a lineage of Cowboys stadiums associated with dramatic rooflines. Outsiders, exactly as this brief warns against, tend to reduce the building to whatever wordmark is currently on it, which is precisely the unstable element.

**Geometry.** Two massive parallel steel arches, nearly 300 feet tall, span end to end over the building and suspend the retractable fabric roof beneath them. A huge rectangular glass walled bowl sits under the arches, with retractable glass doors at both end zones capable of opening the entire end of the building to the outside. A very large center hung video board (screens roughly 160 feet wide by 72 feet tall) is suspended from the arch structure above the field. Silhouette rule: the two parallel arches rising above a boxy glazed bowl are the identifying shape. The arches are the line to draw, never any sponsor wordmark.

**Palette.** Silver to grey structural steel arches, dark glazed glass curtain wall around the bowl, white to light grey retractable roof membrane. Team colors appear throughout interior branding, which should not be reproduced.

**Era markers.** Opened 2009. The building's naming sponsor had already changed at least once by the mid 2020s, underscoring that the architecture, not the signage, is the stable identifier. Date any depiction by roofline and arch structure only.

**Regional.** Arlington, between Dallas and Fort Worth. Recognized statewide and nationally as the Cowboys' home.

**Variants.** Exterior wide shot emphasizing the twin arches against open sky Roof open interior bowl shot with sky visible through the arch structure End zone glass doors open to the outside Night shot with the arches lit and the center video board glowing with generic content, no team wordmark

**Scene role.** Sports business or DFW regional economic stories, engineering and architecture beats. Avoid using as a generic football stinger since this is a specific, ownership sensitive building.

**Flag.** LIVE TRADEMARK. Never render the current or any past naming rights wordmark, and never render the Cowboys' star, an actively licensed NFL team mark. Draw the arches and roofline only, and refer to the building by team and city in narration.

## Fort Worth Stockyards Cattle Drive  [MARK]

**What it is.** A 98 acre historic district on Fort Worth's north side, the surviving core of what was once one of the largest livestock markets in the country, home today to the twice daily Fort Worth Herd cattle drive of Texas Longhorns down the district's brick streets.

**Why it lands.** It is a working performance of real nineteenth century cattle drive culture, mounted drovers in period dress push actual longhorns down a public brick street twice a day, the single most reliable live action Old West image in the state. Outsiders sometimes assume it is a pure theme park recreation rather than a continuously preserved commercial livestock district built on genuine 1900s architecture.

**Geometry.** Red brick paved streets form the drive route, lined by Mission and Spanish Revival brick commercial buildings. The Livestock Exchange Building (an ornate 1902 Mission Revival office building once called the Wall Street of the West) and Cowtown Coliseum (a large, barrel roofed rodeo arena) anchor the district, with an entrance sign or arch spanning the street at one end. The longhorns themselves are the moving foreground element: long bodies and dramatically wide, sweeping horns that can span 8 feet or more tip to tip, in varied coat colors and patterns (red, black, brindle, speckled, roan, no single correct color), driven by mounted drovers in period western wear.

**Palette.** Red brick streets and buildings, white to cream Mission Revival stucco with arched parapets, the longhorns' highly varied hide coloring, dust in the air during an active drive.

**Era markers.** District architecture dates mostly to the 1900s and 1910s. The Herd drive program itself is a modern, 1990s revived heritage performance, so drovers' gear should read as clean, curated western wear rather than authentic 1880s trail dust and grime if the scene is meant to represent today.

**Regional.** Fort Worth. Well known statewide, a signature Fort Worth identity marker distinct from Dallas's glass tower skyline.

**Variants.** Wide shot of longhorns filling the brick street between period buildings Close crop on a single longhorn's head and horn span The Livestock Exchange Building facade alone A mounted drover's silhouette against the building line

**Scene role.** Old West heritage beats, Fort Worth specific establishing shot, tourism and culture segments, a contrast device against Dallas's modern skyline.

**Flag.** Billy Bob's Texas and other named businesses inside the district carry their own separate, live commercial branding. Draw the district's generic brick and Mission Revival architecture, never a specific business's signage.

## Waco Suspension Bridge

**What it is.** A pedestrian suspension bridge over the Brazos River in Waco, opened 1870, the first major suspension bridge in Texas and, at completion, the longest single span suspension bridge west of the Mississippi River, built to move Chisholm Trail cattle drives across the river without swimming the herd.

**Why it lands.** It is a functioning piece of frontier scale engineering, still walkable today, and a hometown pride landmark for Waco distinct from the Baylor and Magnolia (Chip and Joanna Gaines) associations outsiders usually reach for first.

**Geometry.** A single main span of 475 feet between two tall, twin shafted brick towers, each tower built as a pair of piers joined near the top, giving each end a distinctive twin gateway silhouette. Steel suspension cables, originally made by the Roebling company (the firm behind the Brooklyn Bridge), sweep in a classic catenary curve from the tower tops down to a low, flat deck near water level. Silhouette rule: two matching twin towered brick gateways with a shallow cable catenary between them reads as a small scale cousin of the Brooklyn Bridge.

**Palette.** Deep red brick towers, built from nearly 3 million locally made bricks, dark steel cables, the wide brown Brazos River below, riverside park green space on both banks today.

**Era markers.** 1870 original construction. Visible steel truss bracing was added in 1913 to 1914. ## Astrodome  [MARK]

**What it is.** The Harris County Domed Stadium in Houston, opened 1965, the world's first multi purpose domed sports stadium, nicknamed the Eighth Wonder of the World, closed to the public since 2009 and now an empty shell used for storage on the NRG Park grounds.

A simple round drum shaped exterior wall sits below the dome. **Regional.** Houston, NRG Park. One of the most nationally famous stadiums in American sports history, near universal recognition among anyone familiar with 1960s to 1980s sports or space age Americana.

## San Jacinto Monument

**What it is.** A 567.31 foot tapering stone column topped by a 34 foot, 220 ton stone star, built 1936 to 1939 on the San Jacinto Battleground near the Houston Ship Channel to mark the site where Texas won its independence from Mexico in 1836. It is taller than the Washington Monument.

**Why it lands.** Taller than the Washington Monument is another specific, factual, frequently repeated state pride talking point. It also marks the actual battlefield where Texas won, the pride bookend to the Alamo's story of a loss, a pairing every Texas history literate local knows by heart.

**Geometry.** An eight sided, tapering shaft, square in overall cross section with the corners chamfered off, 48 feet square at the base narrowing to 30 feet square near the observation deck. It sits atop a low, wide museum base (a single story building with exhibits and a theater, bronze doors depicting the six historical flags of Texas). A single large star caps the shaft. Silhouette rule: a very slender, sharply tapering, faceted octagonal shaft, more faceted and slender than the Washington Monument's plain four sided taper, with a distinct star shape rather than a simple point at the top, rising from a wide low plinth building rather than open ground.

**Palette.** Pale cream to buff Texas limestone, the same source region as the State Capitol, giving it a warm light color rather than the Washington Monument's cooler grey and white marble. Green battlefield park grounds surround the base; a reflecting pool near the base exists but its current exact configuration is UNCERTAIN, verify before centering a hero shot on it.

**Era markers.** Built 1936 to 1939 for the Texas Centennial, no major exterior change since. The retired World War II battleship Texas was long moored near the monument but was towed away for restoration in 2022, do not include the battleship in a current day depiction without verifying it has returned to the site.

**Regional.** La Porte, Harris County, near the Houston Ship Channel, about 16 miles east of downtown Houston. Well known statewide, a serious history landmark rather than a pop culture one.

**Variants.** Full shaft and star hero shot from the park lawn The star alone as an isolated Texas pride icon Wide shot including the low museum base and grounds The shaft silhouetted against ship channel industrial cranes and tankers, an intentional contrast of history and modern industry

**Geometry.** Ten full size, mid century American cars planted vertically, nose down and tailfins up, at a shared raked angle, arranged in a single evenly spaced row in an open, flat wheat or pasture field with no fence around them. The paint layer changes constantly and should never be treated as a fixed color scheme, only the vertical raked row silhouette is stable.

## Palo Duro Canyon

**What it is.** A 45 mile long canyon carved by the Prairie Dog Town Fork of the Red River into the Caprock Escarpment south of Amarillo, up to 800 to 1,000 feet deep and as much as 20 miles wide at its broadest, the second largest canyon system in the United States, nicknamed the Grand Canyon of Texas.

**Why it lands.** It is a genuine geological surprise in a state most outsiders picture as flat, a canyon that opens up abruptly out of table flat Panhandle farmland with no warning from the highway until you reach the rim. Texans use you-didn't-know-Texas-had-a-canyon-like-this as a specific corrective against outsider assumptions.

**Geometry.** A wide, multicolored canyon with stepped, horizontally banded rock walls, distinct color layers from different rock formations stacked visibly through the cliff faces. The signature formation is the Lighthouse, a hoodoo, a tall narrow standalone rock spire with a slightly bulbous cap, isolated from the canyon wall and reached by a 6 mile round trip trail. Scrubby mesquite and juniper cover the canyon floor and slopes, with the flat tabletop plain of the Llano Estacado visible along the rim above. Silhouette rule: the Lighthouse is the single most drawable icon, a slim vertical rock tower with a slightly wider cap standing alone against the sky, distinct from the surrounding banded cliff walls.

**Palette.** Strongly banded rock colors: red lower slopes (Quartermaster Formation), a middle band of maroon, lavender, gray and white shales with an orange stripe (Tecovas Formation), capped by harder tan to buff sandstone (Trujillo and Ogallala Formations) at the rim. Green grey scrub vegetation, big open sky.

**Era markers.** None, a natural formation. Texas state park infrastructure, roads, and the outdoor amphitheater for the musical drama TEXAS are the only dateable human layer if wanted.

**Regional.** Near Amarillo and Canyon, Texas, in the Texas Panhandle. Well known statewide, the Panhandle's signature natural landmark alongside Cadillac Ranch's built one.

**Variants.** Wide rim view showing the banded canyon walls falling away from flat tableland The Lighthouse formation alone, an isolated vertical rock tower Canyon floor view looking up at striped walls Sunset or sunrise raking light emphasizing the color bands

**Scene role.** Natural wonder and geology beats, outdoor recreation texture, a Texas-isn't-just-flat corrective beat, pairs well with Enchanted Rock or Guadalupe Peak for a landscapes of Texas montage.

## Marfa And Marfa Lights

**What it is.** A small Trans-Pecos railroad town (population roughly 1,700, elevation about 4,685 feet) known both for the unexplained Marfa Lights, small glowing orbs occasionally seen at night from a highway viewing area east of town, and, since 1979, for becoming an unlikely contemporary art destination built around Donald Judd's Chinati Foundation.

**Why it lands.** Marfa carries two entirely different reputations in the same small town at once, an old ranching and railroad town with a genuine, unresolved folk mystery, and a minimalist art pilgrimage site for a completely different crowd. Outsiders usually know only one half, either spooky lights or hipster art town, and are surprised the other exists in the same place.

**Geometry.** The lights themselves: small, point like glowing orbs, usually yellow orange but reported in other colors, seen low over the desert floor beneath the dark shape of the Chinati Mountains, from a paved highway pulloff on US 90 about nine miles east of town. They hover, drift, and are sometimes reported to split or merge. Most conventional explanations point to distant vehicle headlights on US 67 refracted by temperature layered desert air, a mirage effect. Draw them as small, soft edged glowing dots low on a dark horizon, never as anything with defined structure such as a saucer shape or a beam. The town: a low grid of small adobe and wood frame railroad era buildings, the domed 1886 Presidio County Courthouse anchoring downtown, the Chinati Foundation's minimalist concrete and aluminum boxes arranged in open desert grassland at the former Fort D.A. Russell grounds, flat high desert scrubland stretching to distant mountain silhouettes on all sides.

**Palette.** The lights are warm yellow orange glowing dots on near black desert night. The town by day is sunbleached adobe tan, corrugated tin roofing, and a big pale Chihuahuan desert sky. Judd's concrete and aluminum art boxes are cool grey and silver, a deliberate contrast to the warm desert town around them.

**Era markers.** The lights were first documented in 1883 and carry no visual era marker, being a night sky phenomenon. The town's railroad era core dates to the early 1900s. The Chinati Foundation's presence from 1979 onward is the modern art town era marker, distinguishing old Marfa from art world Marfa depictions.

**Regional.** Presidio County, far West Texas (Trans-Pecos), between the Davis Mountains and Big Bend. The lights are known statewide and somewhat nationally as an unsolved mystery reference point; the art scene is known more narrowly but is growing in design and travel press.

**Variants.** Night highway pulloff shot, small glowing orbs low over a dark desert silhouette The domed Presidio County Courthouse by day A Judd concrete box installation in open desert grass Wide high desert landscape shot establishing the isolation and emptiness around the town

**Scene role.** Mystery and unexplained phenomena beats (handled as folklore, never staged as a literal UFO claim), contemporary art in unlikely places beats, far West Texas regional identity, night sky and stargazing texture.

**Flag.** None, though the lights should be presented with the scientific explanations on record rather than as a confirmed paranormal claim, to avoid misleading viewers.

## Enchanted Rock

**What it is.** A pink granite dome, the exposed top of a much larger buried batholith roughly 1.08 billion years old, rising about 425 feet above the surrounding Texas Hill Country floor near Fredericksburg, the largest such pink granite dome in the United States, held sacred by Tonkawa, Apache and Comanche peoples.

**Why it lands.** It is a Hill Country rite of passage day hike, a bare rock summit scramble accessible to nearly anyone, so it functions as a common family or school trip landmark. The folklore, ghost fires and creaking sounds reported at night from the rock's thermal contraction, gives it a genuine goosebump layer beyond just a nice view. Outsiders sometimes picture a small hill rather than a single unbroken dome of exposed bedrock, geologically comparable in kind, if not scale, to a dome like Georgia's Stone Mountain.

**Geometry.** A broad, smoothly rounded, mostly bare dome of pink granite, elevation 1,825 feet above sea level, rising about 425 feet above its surroundings, covering roughly 640 acres. The surface exfoliates in large, curved, sheet like plates, a visible layered or onion skin cracking pattern typical of granite domes. Scattered scrub oak and prickly pear grow in shallow soil pockets on the lower slopes, with bare rock dominating the upper dome. Silhouette rule: a single, smooth, continuous rounded hump breaking the Hill Country's low rolling scrubland horizon, no sharp peak, cliff or spire. The roundness and bareness together, not height alone, are what read as Enchanted Rock rather than a generic hill.

**Palette.** Pink to salmon granite, the defining color, visibly warmer and pinker than plain grey granite, especially at sunrise or sunset. Dark grey green scrub oak and cedar at the base, pale blue Hill Country sky.

**Era markers.** None, a natural formation. A small state natural area parking and trailhead area is the only human layer.

**Regional.** Between Fredericksburg and Llano in the Texas Hill Country. Very well known statewide, especially in Central Texas, as a hiking and camping destination.

**Variants.** Wide shot of the bare rounded dome rising above Hill Country scrubland Close texture shot of the exfoliating granite sheets Hikers small on the open summit for scale Sunset shot with the granite glowing distinctly pink to salmon

**Scene role.** Hill Country outdoor recreation beats, geology and natural wonder texture, folklore and legend beats handled as Indigenous oral tradition and treated respectfully rather than as spooky kitsch, family outdoors lifestyle beats.

**Flag.** Handle the folklore respectfully as living Indigenous oral tradition tied to a site multiple nations consider sacred, not as generic haunted-rock kitsch.

## Guadalupe Peak El Capitan  [MARK]

**What it is.** Guadalupe Peak (8,751 feet, the highest point in Texas) and its neighboring cliff El Capitan (8,064 feet), both part of the Guadalupe Mountains, an exposed ancient Permian fossil reef rising more than 3,000 feet directly out of the Chihuahuan Desert floor in far West Texas.

**Why it lands.** The highest point in Texas is a specific state superlative fact Texans reach for, but the visually famous element is actually the lower of the two peaks, El Capitan's sheer cliff face, because it sits right beside US Highway 62/180 and has been the dominant landmark for travelers, first stagecoach, then highway, for more than 150 years. Outsiders who know the highest point in Texas fact often picture the wrong summit, since Guadalupe Peak itself sits set back from the road and reads as far less dramatic from a driving vantage than El Capitan's sheer face.

**Geometry.** A mountain massif rising abruptly out of flat desert with no foothills to soften the transition. El Capitan presents a sheer, near vertical, pale limestone cliff face on three sides, reading from the highway as a blunt, prow like end on profile. Guadalupe Peak sits just behind and above it as the true high point, but reads as a broader, rounded summit rather than a dramatic cliff from the same road. Silhouette rule: draw El Capitan's flat, sheer faced prow, like a ship's bow or a blunt wall, as the dominant foreground shape for any highway or travel angle, since that is the shape that is actually famous. Reserve a true summit peak silhouette for Guadalupe Peak only when the story is specifically about the highest-point-in-Texas fact.

**Palette.** Pale grey tan to white Permian reef limestone, darker desert scrub (creosote, agave, sotol) at the base. The rock can flush pink orange at sunset, a stark contrast between the pale stone mass and the flat tan desert floor and big sky around it.

**Era markers.** None for the geology itself. A stainless steel pyramid marker on Guadalupe Peak's summit, placed 1958 by American Airlines to mark the Butterfield Overland Mail centennial, is a dateable man made summit detail if a close summit shot is wanted.

**Regional.** Guadalupe Mountains National Park, far West Texas (Culberson and Hudspeth counties), near the New Mexico line. Well known statewide as a superlative even among people who have never visited, and well known to travelers on US 62/180 between El Paso and Carlsbad Caverns.

**Variants.** Highway eye view of El Capitan's sheer, prow like face dominating the horizon The two peaks together silhouette, El Capitan's flat face beside Guadalupe Peak's higher, rounded summit Close detail of the exposed reef limestone cliff texture Sunset shot with the cliff face flushed pink orange against a darkening sky

**Why it lands.** It is the one place a Texan can point to and say the exact geographic border between the US and Mexico is a specific, walkable river you can stand beside with a foreign country visible on the opposite bank at arm's reach, a tangible version of an otherwise abstract political line. **Geometry.** Santa Elena Canyon specifically: sheer, nearly vertical limestone canyon walls, with reported wall heights in the range of roughly 1,500 feet by some measurements (figures vary by measurement point and source, treat any single precise number as approximate and UNCERTAIN for a specific spot). The Mexico side wall sits to the river's left, the Texas side to the river's right. The river itself here is a modest, often shallow, silty green brown ribbon, small relative to the towering rock. General Big Bend canyon character: nearly vertical limestone walls cut through raised uplift blocks, with the Chisos Mountains, the only mountain range entirely inside a US national park, visible as a separate jagged sky island silhouette inland from the river canyons. Silhouette rule: a single narrow, dark, vertical slot cut into an otherwise unbroken, long, flat topped cliff wall, seen frontally across flat desert scrub, is Santa Elena's specific and most recognizable shape.

**Palette.** Pale tan grey limestone canyon walls, with darker desert varnish streaking on the rock in places. Muddy green brown river water, scrubby riverside cane and willow green at the waterline. The Chisos Mountains read as a cooler blue grey silhouette in the distance.

**Era markers.** None, a natural and geological feature. The international boundary here is unfenced river, not a built wall, worth stating plainly if a scene touches on border topics, rather than defaulting to fence or wall imagery that simply does not apply to this stretch.

**Regional.** Big Bend National Park, the far southern tip of West Texas. Very well known statewide as Texas's premier wilderness landmark, moderately known nationally as a remote, dramatic national park.

## Texas Courthouse Squares

**What it is.** The county courthouse on a square town plan used across most of Texas's 254 counties, a substantial, architecturally significant courthouse building set at the center of a public square with businesses facing inward around it, built in waves of style from Greek Revival through Second Empire, Romanesque Revival, Beaux-Arts Classicism and Art Deco Moderne.

**Why it lands.** Nearly every Texan has a specific hometown or nearby county courthouse in mind when this is mentioned. It is less a single landmark than a recognizable type that repeats county to county, giving small town Texas its visual rhythm, drive into almost any county seat and the courthouse tower is the first thing visible over the rooflines. Outsiders tend to picture one generic old courthouse rather than realizing Texas has one of the largest and most stylistically varied collections of historic courthouses in the country, actively protected by a dedicated state preservation program.

**Geometry.** A substantial multi story masonry building, often with a corner or central clock tower or dome, sited in the middle of a public square block, with streets and low commercial storefronts framing the square on all four sides, facing inward toward the courthouse. Symmetrical massing, with entrances on all four sides giving equal prominence to each. Style varies by construction era: early Republic and antebellum courthouses are simple Greek Revival with a hipped roof and cupola; post Civil War Victorian courthouses use Second Empire mansard roofs and Italianate bracketed cornices, more ornate; turn of the century courthouses use Romanesque Revival round arches and rusticated stone, or Renaissance Revival and Beaux-Arts Classicism with columns, pediments and a dome; 1930s onward courthouses shift to Moderne, clean, stripped, vertical lines with minimal ornament. Silhouette rule: across every era, the one constant is a taller central mass, tower, dome, or simply extra stories, rising above a symmetrical block, standing alone in the middle of an open square rather than sharing a block wall like an ordinary downtown building. That isolated, elevated placement is what reads as a Texas courthouse regardless of style.

**Palette.** Varies by era and region: warm tan or buff limestone common in Central Texas, red brick common in East Texas, grey granite or sandstone elsewhere, red tile or dark slate or metal roofing on towers, green lawn, and often a war memorial or monument somewhere on the square grounds.

**Era markers.** The architectural style itself is the era marker, see the style breakdown above, a fast way to date a scene without any other prop.

**Regional.** Statewide, one per county seat across 254 counties, a genuinely ubiquitous small town Texas pattern rather than a single site to visit.

**Variants.** A Romanesque Revival tower and square composition, the most postcard familiar version, as the default A plainer Greek Revival cupola version for an earlier era scene A Moderne Art Deco version for a 1930s scene Wide establishing shot of the square itself, storefronts facing inward, the courthouse rising in the center

**Scene role.** Default small town Texas establishing shot, local government or county news beats, any story that needs to signal this is a specific Texas county seat without inventing a real building, historical architecture segments.

**Flag.** None; this is a building type, not a single site.

**Why it lands.** The Spurs are a source of unusually durable, low drama civic pride in San Antonio, five championships built around one long tenured roster era, a franchise the city never feared losing, so the arena is treated as a genuine community gathering place rather than only a sports venue. ## Big Tex State Fair  [MARK]

**What it is.** A 55 foot tall cowboy figure at Fair Park in Dallas, the marketing icon and greeter of the State Fair of Texas since 1952, rebuilt after a 2012 electrical fire destroyed the original figure's clothing, face and hat.

**Why it lands.** Howdy Folks, Big Tex's booming recorded greeting, is a specific, beloved sound every Texan who grew up going to the State Fair can recall instantly, a genuine once a year seasonal ritual marker rather than a permanent tourist photo op. Outsiders often do not grasp the scale, roughly five stories tall, until they see a human figure standing at his boot for comparison, and do not know he is a specific, rebuilt after tragedy figure with real local mourning attached to the 2012 fire.

**Geometry.** A giant standing male figure in classic cowboy dress: a broad brimmed hat (75 gallon size), a plaid or patterned western shirt (the current shirt has a 14 foot collar and 23 foot sleeves), blue denim jeans (the current pair has a 27 foot waist), and oversized boots, typically with one arm raised in a greeting wave. Built on a steel truss skeleton under a fiberglass skin. Silhouette rule: an enormous, static, single standing cowboy figure with an oversized hat is the shape; scale relative to the fairground crowd and rides below him is what sells the size in any drawing.

**Palette.** Blue denim jeans, a plaid or patterned western shirt (the specific colorway has varied across rebuilds, treat the exact current scheme as UNCERTAIN and verify current photo reference), brown boots and hat, a weathered, sun bleached tone appropriate for an outdoor fiberglass figure that stands year round.

**Era markers.** The original figure stood from 1952 to 2012. The 2012 fire and 2013 rebuild is a fixed, dateable event, pre-2012 depictions show the older proportions and face, post-2013 depictions show the current rebuilt figure. The fair itself runs a specific autumn window each year, late September into October, a seasonal rather than architectural marker.

**Regional.** Fair Park, Dallas. Extremely well known statewide as the symbol of the State Fair of Texas, moderately known nationally.

**Variants.** Full figure wide shot against the Fair Park midway with rides in the background Close crop on the face and hat for a greeting beat Small human figures at his boots for scale contrast Night shot lit against fairground lights

**Scene role.** Autumn or State Fair seasonal hook, Dallas civic pride beats, scale based visual jokes, nostalgia beats around the 2012 fire and community rebuilding.

**Flag.** LIVE TRADEMARK. Big Tex is an actively used marketing mark of the State Fair of Texas, with its own dedicated domain and branding program. Draw the generic giant cowboy figure form, or use specifically for a State Fair story, do not present as a free standing generic mascot for unrelated use.

## Sam Houston Statue Huntsville

**What it is.** A 67 foot tall statue of Sam Houston, first president of the Republic of Texas, titled A Tribute to Courage and nicknamed Big Sam, sculpted by David Adickes in 1994 and installed beside Interstate 45 at Huntsville, roughly halfway between Houston and Dallas.

**Why it lands.** It is a genuine startle-the-first-time-you-see-it roadside monument, visible from the freeway well before the exit, the kind of landmark Texans use to time road trips, we're at Sam Houston, halfway there. Outsiders often assume any giant roadside statue in Texas must be a kitsch novelty piece and miss that this one specifically honors a serious historical figure, a Texas revolution general and republic president, at monumental civic scale, not a joke.

**Geometry.** A single standing male figure in period frontier and statesman dress, positioned facing the highway. **Era markers.** Installed 1994, a fixed date, with no significant redesign since. A 2008 graffiti vandalism incident is a minor historical footnote, not a visual era marker.

**Regional.** Huntsville, on Interstate 45 between Houston and Dallas. Well known statewide as a highway waypoint, a specifically Texas driving culture landmark more than a standalone tourist destination.
