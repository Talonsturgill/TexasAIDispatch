# The regions — ten Texases, and each one has its own green

**The law this file exists to enforce: a Texan forgives a stylized drawing and does not forgive
being told they live somewhere they don't.** Style is free. Place is not.

The ten vegetational areas are Gould, Hoffman and Rechenthin, *Vegetational Areas of Texas*, Texas
A&M Agricultural Experiment Station Leaflet 492, 1960, still the classification Texas Parks and
Wildlife publishes as the Gould Ecoregions map **[V]**. We use them because they are citable,
because they are what a Texan's eye actually sorts on, and because a story's county maps to
exactly one of them.

**Every scene declares its region before any colour is chosen.** The biome module takes a region
name, not a mood.

---

## How to read a region entry

`light` is the single most important field. It is what makes a Panhandle frame unmistakably not a
Houston frame even when both contain a data centre. `ground` is the second, because the ground
plane fills the bottom third of most of our compositions.

Marks: **[V]** verified against a cited source, **[U]** unverified, verify before it ships.

---

## 1. High Plains (the Panhandle)

**Light.** Enormous and flat. The sky is the subject and the land is a line under it. Sun is
high and unfiltered, shadows are short and hard at midday, and the horizon is genuinely straight
for most of the frame width. Dust hazes the low sky brown in spring.

**Ground.** Caliche and red-brown loam. Section roads run true north to south, so a road in frame
should be dead straight and meet the horizon at a point.

**Vegetation.** Shortgrass, almost no trees except planted windbreaks and cottonwoods at
farmsteads. A lone tree is a landmark, not scenery.

**Built.** Grain elevators as the tallest thing for thirty miles. Centre-pivot irrigation circles.
Wind turbines in ranks with synchronised red aviation lights at night **[V]**. Feedlots.

**Our beat.** Wind, the Ogallala aquifer under everything, and data centre siting on cheap flat
land near transmission. **This is the region where the newest industry sits on the oldest
landscape most starkly**, and that contrast is our best recurring composition.

**Palette note.** Ochre, dust-brown, bleached sky. Nothing lush. If a Panhandle frame reads green,
it is wrong.

---

## 2. Rolling Plains

**Light.** Similar hardness to the High Plains but the land breaks up. Red dirt takes the light
warm, so shadow sides go violet.

**Ground.** Red beds. Genuinely red soil, which is a gift for a palette and a trap for a lazy one.

**Vegetation.** Mesquite everywhere, and mesquite is **low, crooked, wide and lacy** **[V]** —
never a generic round tree. Juniper. Shinnery oak.

**Built.** Pumpjacks, working and abandoned. Barbed wire on cedar posts that lean.

**Our beat.** Older oilfield, wind, and the transmission corridors that cross it.

---

## 3. Cross Timbers and Prairies

**Light.** The transition belt. Broken oak woodland alternating with prairie, so light is dappled
where the timber is and open where it is not.

**Ground.** Sandy over limestone, tan to grey.

**Vegetation.** Post oak and blackjack oak in dense strips. This is the region that reads as
"woods" to a north Texan and as "scrub" to an east Texan.

**Built.** Fort Worth is the anchor. Stockyards, rail, and the DFW sprawl pushing west.

**Our beat.** DFW data centre corridor, and the point where metro demand meets ranch land.

---

## 4. Blackland Prairie

**Light.** Open, humid, softer than the plains. Summer light goes milky by afternoon.

**Ground.** **Black, cracking clay.** It is genuinely black when wet and pale grey-tan when dry,
and it cracks into a polygonal pattern in drought that is one of the most drawable ground textures
in the state **[V]**.

**Vegetation.** Almost all farmed now. Tallgrass remnants. Bois d'arc and pecan along the creeks.

**Built.** The I-35 corridor. Austin to Dallas. Courthouse squares in the county seats.

**Our beat.** Policy, because Austin is here. The pink Capitol dome is a Blackland frame.

---

## 5. Post Oak Savannah

**Light.** Warm, humid, filtered through open canopy. The gentlest light in the state.

**Ground.** Sandy loam, tan.

**Vegetation.** Scattered post oak over grass, genuinely savannah-shaped: individual trees with
space between them, not a closed canopy.

**Built.** Small towns, ranchettes, and increasingly exurban Houston and Austin overflow.

---

## 6. Piney Woods

**Light.** Closed canopy. Vertical shafts. The only region in Texas where the light comes down in
columns rather than across.

**Ground.** Needle litter, red-orange clay road cuts, standing water in the bottoms.

**Vegetation.** Loblolly and shortleaf pine, tall and straight, with hardwood in the creek
bottoms. Trees are TALL here, which is the compositional difference: verticals dominate.

**Built.** Timber, paper mills, oil and gas. Lufkin, Nacogdoches, Tyler.

**Our beat.** East Texas gas, and the fact that the state's water is here while the demand is
west. **Water is the argument underneath most Texas energy stories and almost nobody draws it.**

---

## 7. Gulf Prairies and Marshes

**Light.** Humid, hazy, high glare. Sky often white rather than blue. Salt haze softens
everything past the middle distance, which is a genuine atmospheric-perspective gift.

**Ground.** Flat, wet, black to grey. Standing water, canals, spoil banks.

**Vegetation.** Cordgrass, spartina, live oak deformed by the prevailing wind into a permanent
lean **[V]**. Palms are planted, not native, and reading as tropical is a mistake.

**Built.** **The petrochemical corridor**, and it is the most cinematic industrial landscape in
the state: flare stacks, cracking towers, pipe racks, tank farms, ship channel cranes. Houston.

**Our beat.** Petrochemical AI, the ship channel, hurricane risk to the grid, and cooling water.

---

## 8. South Texas Plains (the Brush Country)

**Light.** Hard, bright, dusty. Long dry season. Heat shimmer is real and constant.

**Ground.** Caliche, sand, thorn litter.

**Vegetation.** Thorn scrub. Mesquite, huisache, blackbrush, prickly pear. **Prickly pear with
magenta tunas standing upright on the pad rims** **[U]**. Dense enough to be genuinely
impassable, which is why it is called brush country.

**Built.** Ranching at enormous scale, Eagle Ford wells, border infrastructure, colonias.

**Our beat.** Eagle Ford, the border, and water. Handle colonias and border communities with the
care in `CULTURE.md`, never as scenery.

---

## 9. Edwards Plateau (the Hill Country)

**Light.** Clear, dry, high-contrast. Limestone bounces light UP into shadows, so shadows here are
brighter and cooler than anywhere else in the state. This is the single most distinctive lighting
behaviour in Texas and almost nobody draws it.

**Ground.** **White to pale grey limestone**, exposed constantly. Rock is the ground, not soil.

**Vegetation.** Ashe juniper (locally "cedar"), live oak, and the springs. Green here is grey-green
and blue-green, never yellow-green.

**Built.** Ranch gates, low-water crossings, dance halls, county courthouses on squares.

**Our beat.** San Antonio and Austin data centres, the Edwards Aquifer, and the springs that
depend on it.

---

## 10. Trans-Pecos

**Light.** The cleanest air in Texas. Enormous visual range, so distant mountains stay saturated
instead of hazing out, which INVERTS the usual atmospheric perspective rule. Sunsets are long and
the alpenglow on rock is the signature.

**Ground.** Rock, gravel, sparse desert pavement. Volcanic and limestone both.

**Vegetation.** Creosote, lechuguilla, ocotillo, sotol, yucca. Sparse and individual: every plant
has space around it and reads as a silhouette.

**Built.** Almost nothing, which is the point. Marfa, Alpine, Van Horn. The Permian's western
edge. Observatories, because of the dark sky.

**Our beat.** The Permian, the dark-sky fight against industrial lighting, and the fact that this
is where the grid is thinnest.

**Palette note.** This is the region the brand's Big Bend dusk palette comes from, so it is the
one most at risk of being used as the default everywhere. **Guard it.** If every Dispatch looks
Trans-Pecos, the library has failed.

---

## The rule that ties this file to the engine

`biomes.tsx` exposes one region per entry, and a scene declares its region from the STORY'S
COUNTY, not from what would look good. If the county is in Harris, the frame is humid Gulf light
over black clay, and the pretty desert palette does not get to visit.

When a story genuinely spans regions, cut between them and let the light do the work. That
contrast is free storytelling and it is true.
