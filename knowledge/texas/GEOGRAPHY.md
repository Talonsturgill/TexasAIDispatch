# Geography — how the Texas land actually works, and how it dates a frame

`REGIONS.md` names the ten regions and their light. This file is the deeper research: how the
terrain, water, vegetation and survey lines actually behave, because getting them right places a
frame more precisely than any landmark, and getting them wrong lands a scene hundreds of miles
from where it belongs. Every entry leads with **the mistake**, because the mistake is the default
an outsider draws.

The facts here are measured and carry their numbers on purpose. A caption that needs one asks a
computation for it (the compute-not-generate law); these are the reference the computation checks
against, not values to retype into prose.

---

## Quick reference the engine can reach for

- **Rain follows longitude, not height.** The measured gradient is **4.10 inches of annual rain per
  degree of longitude eastward**, r-squared 0.904, about **6.9 inches per 100 miles**. Del Rio
  (under the tallest Balcones relief) takes 19.52 inches; Boerne, 200 miles east under a smaller
  step, takes 38.10. Relief and rainfall are independent variables here.
- **Water color is decided by rock, not cleanliness**, and it places a scene faster than a landmark:
  - Edwards spring water — **jade green**, white limestone bottom showing through
  - Brazos, Trinity — **opaque milk-coffee brown**
  - Red River — **genuinely orange** (Permian redbed soil)
  - Sabine, Neches — **near-black** with tannin
  - open Gulf near the coast — **brown-green** from river sediment, never blue
- **Exactly one Texas city has mountains on its horizon:** El Paso (the Franklin Mountains,
  7,192 ft, run through it). Amarillo, Lubbock, Dallas and Houston have nothing on the horizon.
  Austin and San Antonio have hills on the WEST side of the fault only and dead-flat ground east.
- **The fence line is the cheapest dating device in the reference.** Barbed wire arrived in the
  mid-1870s; enclosure was practically complete by 1885. After that nearly every landscape has a
  ruled line of posts, and the grass differs on each side. Before it, cattle and grass run to the
  horizon uninterrupted.

## What an artist gets wrong about Texas terrain

**Rolling hills everywhere.** The default mental picture is gently undulating grassland with a lone
tree on a rise. That shape is honest in maybe a fifth of the state and false in the rest. On the
Llano Estacado the horizon is ruled straight edge to edge. The whole tableland falls about 1.89
metres per kilometre to the southeast and no viewer can see a slope that gentle. A Panhandle frame
with rolling hills has drawn the wrong 32,000 square miles.

**Treating relief and rainfall as the same variable.** They are independent in Texas and the state
proves it twice. Del Rio sits under the tallest section of the Balcones Escarpment at roughly 1,000
feet of relief and takes 19.52 inches of rain. Boerne sits under a much smaller step 200 miles east
and takes 38.10. Green does not follow height here, it follows longitude (4.10 inches per degree
east, about 6.9 per 100 miles).

**Hill Country limestone in the wrong county.** Reeves County is a flat desert plain at 2,610 feet
with 12.08 inches of rain, salt cedar, mesquite, oilfield and mountains only as a distant blue line.
It has no pale stepped limestone benches, no bald cypress and no clear green water. Put Hill Country
there and the frame is 400 miles off.

**Making all Texas water blue.** Water colour here is decided by rock, not cleanliness, and it
places a scene faster than any landmark. Spring water off the Edwards is jade green with a white
limestone bottom showing through. The Brazos and the Trinity are opaque milk-coffee brown. The Red
River is genuinely orange because it carries Permian redbed soil. The Sabine and the Neches are
near-black with tannin. One blue for all of them throws away the most useful signal available.

**Drawing the Hill Country as a mountain range.** It is a dissected plateau. Every hilltop stands at
roughly the same height because every hilltop is a leftover piece of one flat surface that streams
cut into. If the summits in a frame do not line up, the frame is not the Hill Country.

**Skipping the two-layer vegetation and the date it implies.** A modern Hill Country slope is a
closed dark Ashe juniper thicket with bare soil under it. The same slope before 1850 was open grass
with juniper confined to rocky ground. TSHA names the causes as the end of prairie fires,
overgrazing, wagon trains, trail drives and drought. Mesquite tells the same story at scale. It once
grew only along streams and in open groves and it now occupies fifty to sixty million acres. A frame
set in 1840 with a mesquite savannah is a frame set in 1940.

**Waving tallgrass on the Blackland Prairie.** Less than 1 percent of the original Blackland prairie
vegetation remains, some estimates under 0.1 percent. The honest modern image is dark, almost black
clay in wide low fields, cracked into polygons in summer and sticky after rain. The surviving
prairie is a fenced rectangle that looks like an error in the pattern.

**Forgetting the fence line.** Barbed wire arrived in the mid-1870s and by the end of the century
enclosure was practically complete. After 1885 nearly every Texas landscape has a ruled line of
posts in it, and the grass on one side looks different from the grass on the other. Before that the
cattle and the grass run to the horizon uninterrupted. A fence is the cheapest and most reliable
dating device in the whole reference.

**Drawing the same parcel geometry statewide.** South Texas was surveyed into long thin strips with
narrow frontage on a watercourse because irrigation decided everything (some 170 porciones are
recorded). The Panhandle was laid out later by rectangular survey on land where no river governed
anything, so it is a compass-aligned grid with roads on the section lines every mile. Reverse those
two and a scene lands 600 miles from where it belongs.

**Making the Trans-Pecos one colour.** The Guadalupe Mountains are pale grey Permian reef limestone.
The Davis and Chisos ranges are dark volcanic rock from eruptions about 35 million years ago.
Painting both a generic desert tan loses the one contrast that makes far West Texas legible. The
other giveaway is the bajada, the long straight faintly concave debris apron sweeping from the range
front to the basin floor. Get that curve wrong and nothing else in the frame saves it.

**Making the Gulf look like an ocean.** The Laguna Madre runs 130 miles and is eight to fourteen
inches deep across much of it. Draw it as a mirror with wading birds far from any shore. The open
Gulf near the Texas coast is brown-green from river sediment rather than blue, and Padre Island is
at most three miles wide with a dune belt 25 to 40 feet high.

**Assuming a dust storm looks like weather.** On the High Plains it is a vertical wall against a
ruled horizon because there is no terrain to break it up. It has a hard defined front, tan at the
base and darker above. Ordinary frames from those years need drifted soil banked against fence lines
like snow.

**Putting mountains behind a city that has none.** El Paso genuinely has the Franklin Mountains
running through it at 7,192 feet. Amarillo, Lubbock, Dallas and Houston have nothing on the horizon
at all. Austin and San Antonio have hills on one side only, west of the fault, and dead flat ground
on the other. A skyline backed by peaks is right for exactly one Texas city.

**Missing the canyon.** Palo Duro is 800 feet deep, up to 120 miles long and six to twenty miles
wide, and it is invisible until the rim. It is a hole in a flat plane, not a valley between hills.
The same is true of the Caprock generally. The drama is the edge, not the height.

**Ignoring what the springs mean.** Barton, San Marcos and Comal exist because the Balcones fault
zone interrupts the water-bearing rock under the plateau and pushes water up under artesian
pressure. Each one has a city on it, and that is the whole reason those cities are where they are. A
river that starts full size out of the ground with nothing upstream is a real Texas image and it
belongs only on that line.

## The move this doc enables

Pick the region first, then let terrain, water color, vegetation era and survey geometry all agree
with it. A frame where the water is jade, the summits line up flat, and a fence rules the middle
distance is unmistakably post-1885 Hill Country, and it got there without a single caption. That is
the whole point: the land is a location system, and drawing it right is cheaper and more convincing
than naming the place.
